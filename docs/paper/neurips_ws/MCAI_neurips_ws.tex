\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as task planners for
embodied agents, yet long prerequisite chains remain a weak point.  We study
this in offline plan generation for a Minecraft agent, scoring each plan with
a rule-based validator that walks it over a virtual inventory.  For five of
the seven LLMs we test (8\,B to 671\,B parameters where disclosed), plans for
two-chain (L2) tasks pass only 10--30\% of the time without retrieval, against
45--95\% on single-material tasks.  Adding three same-category demonstrations
to the prompt improves all five.  In a four-condition ablation on one model,
randomly chosen same-category demonstrations recover most of the gain ($+$31
percentage points; $+$20 when the scorer also charges consumed materials), and
ranking them by relevance over the game's recipe graph adds a smaller increment
over lexical ranking ($+$12 points) that neither our pre-specified correction
nor the stricter scorer confirms.  Demonstration content is also inherited
verbatim: an invalid command in the combat demonstrations appears in 71.5\% of
retrieval trials on those tasks and in none of 505 trials without retrieval.
In this setting, adding same-category demonstrations to a shared rule text
matters more than selecting them precisely, and their defects have to be
checked because they are copied.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An LLM that plans for a virtual embodied agent needs long chains of
procedural, version-specific knowledge:
which tool mines which block, what a recipe consumes, which intermediate item
must exist first.  A symbolic planner is handed this knowledge as a domain
model; an LLM planner is assumed to have absorbed it in pretraining.
Minecraft makes the difference measurable.  Frontier models decompose
single-step goals readily~\citep{wang2023voyager,wang2023describe}, and
Plancraft~\citep{dagan2024plancraft} finds that they struggle with multi-step
crafting.  We ask which facet of that knowledge an LLM planner must be
\emph{given} rather than assumed to hold: its existence in the prompt as worked examples, its
relevance to the query, its correctness, or the model's capacity to use it.

\textbf{Scope.}  This is a study of LLM \emph{plan generation} with an
embodied motivation.  Every confirmatory result comes from scoring generated
plans offline: a rule-based validator walks each plan over a virtual inventory
whose initial state is given by construction, with no perception, symbol
grounding or execution.  A live-execution study does not separate the
conditions and is reported as a limitation, not as evidence
(\secref{sec:limits}).  The neuro-symbolic interaction we evaluate is
therefore \emph{pre-generation knowledge supply}: the \texttt{minecraft-data}
recipe graph drives the ranking of the demonstrations that enter the prompt.
In the static study the validator serves only as the metric
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).  Every condition shares a fixed rule text with the
tool tiers and one generic chain (\appref{app:prompt}), so no retrieval is not a
blank prompt.

We make three contributions, each tied to the component and experiment that
supports it.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{A diagnosis.}  Without retrieval, five of the seven models we
  test pass only 10--30\% of two-chain (L2) tasks against 45--95\% of
  single-material (L1) tasks, and 74--95\% of their failing tasks carry a
  prerequisite that is never acquired (no retrieval; Table~\ref{tab:main},
  \appref{app:failmodes}).  Two open-weight models do not show the gap, and we
  have no tested explanation for why.
  \item \textbf{A decomposition of the remedy.}  Same-category demonstrations
  improve all five affected models, the 8B one included
  (Table~\ref{tab:main}).  On the ablation model, random demonstrations
  recover most of the gain, and ranking them with SAER (State-Aware Example Retrieval), a training-free scorer
  over the recipe graph, adds a smaller increment that our tests do not
  confirm (demonstration retrieval, with SAER's ranking the one step that reads
  the graph; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, \appref{app:mcnemar}).
  \item \textbf{A measurement of defect inheritance.}  Planners copy what their
  demonstrations contain, including an unregistered command and short
  quantities, at rates near zero without retrieval (\secref{sec:knowledge_gap}, \appref{app:demoquality}).  This is the
  correctness facet's mechanism.
\end{enumerate}
Each effect is reported under the pre-specified scorer and under a stricter
one that also charges consumed materials.  The existence effect survives both;
the relevance increment survives neither the pre-specified correction nor the
stricter scorer.

\section{Related Work}

\noindent\textbf{LLM agents and retrieval in Minecraft.}
Minecraft is an established testbed for open-ended LLM
agents~\citep{fan2022minedojo}: lifelong learners
(Voyager~\citep{wang2023voyager}) and open-world multi-task planners
(DEPS~\citep{wang2023describe}, GITM~\citep{zhu2023ghost},
JARVIS-1~\citep{wang2023jarvis}), all dependent on frontier-scale
models.  We build on MINDcraft~\citep{nottingham2023mindcraft}.  Closest
to our mechanism, \citet{leung2025knowledge} propose Goal-Oriented
Graphs (GoGs), which recursively retrieve a goal's prerequisites from a
dependency graph;
Plan4MC~\citep{yuan2023plan4mc} traverses a pre-built skill prerequisite
graph over RL skills; Optimus-1~\citep{li2024optimus} retrieves world
knowledge from a hierarchical knowledge graph; and
Plancraft~\citep{dagan2024plancraft} is a crafting-GUI benchmark whose
oracle planner finds LLMs struggle with multi-step crafting.  Unlike these, we retrieve few-shot
\emph{demonstrations} to condition plan generation, decomposing
\emph{which} facet of knowledge provision binds performance.

\noindent\textbf{Retrieval-augmented in-context learning.}
\label{sec:ricl}
In-context learning (ICL)~\citep{brown2020language} adapts LLMs from
demonstrations without parameter updates; \citet{min2022rethinking} show
format, not label correctness, drives ICL, motivating SAER's focus on
structural relevance.  Retrieval-based ICL selects examples per query:
unsupervised methods (BM25~\citep{robertson2009probabilistic},
SBERT~\citep{reimers2019sentence}) use lexical/semantic similarity;
EPR~\citep{rubin2022learning} and LLM-R~\citep{wang2024learning} train
retrievers from LLM feedback; InstructRAG~\citep{wang2025instructrag}
retrieves over an instruction graph; broader
RAG~\citep{lewis2020retrieval,gao2023retrieval} augments facts rather than
task demonstrations.  SAER is \emph{training-free} (unlike EPR/LLM-R) and
reads material-tier similarity off the \texttt{minecraft-data} crafting
graph, selecting demonstrations rather than subgoals with no traversal at
inference.  BM25 is our lexical baseline
(\secref{sec:models}); SBERT appears in \appref{app:threeway}.

\noindent\textbf{Planning beyond Minecraft and across scales.}
Embodied planning beyond Minecraft~\citep{huang2022language} has
explored complementary directions: SayCan~\citep{ahn2022can} grounds
actions in affordances, LLM+P~\citep{liu2023llmp} delegates to a PDDL
planner, \citet{guan2023leveraging} have the LLM construct the PDDL
domain model itself under validator feedback, and
Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion} refines plans via
post-execution self-evaluation.  The LLM-Modulo
position~\citep{kambhampati2024position} generalises this family: LLMs as
approximate knowledge sources paired with external model-based
verifiers.  Our validator plays that role only at runtime; the intervention we study is
\emph{pre}-generation, supplying symbolic knowledge rather than checking the
output.  None of these addresses
how much of that knowledge must be supplied, or how the answer varies
with scale.  \citet{shi2023large} show irrelevant
context distracts models (motivating our category filter);
\citet{liu2024lost} document a mid-prompt position bias.  PlanBench~\citep{valmeekam2023planbench} argues
commonsense benchmarks conflate planning with retrieval, and
\citet{xu2026depthceiling} attribute multi-step failure to a
\emph{reasoning-depth} ceiling that scale and few-shot prompting barely
move.  We isolate another constraint---stating the rules is not enough, and
the binding limit is procedural knowledge in \emph{task-matched} form---and an
exploratory thinking-mode ablation (\appref{app:thinking}) is consistent with
test-time reasoning raising L1 without unlocking L2.

\section{Method}

\subsection{System Overview}

same-category demonstrations, the planning LLM writes a step plan, a plan
validator checks its prerequisites (and at runtime may repair it), and an
executor turns the steps into Mineflayer actions (\appref{sec:exec};
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\noindent\textbf{Why demonstrations rather than the graph.}  The recipe graph
could be handed to the model directly, as Goal-Oriented
Graphs~\citep{leung2025knowledge} do, or to a classical planner, which can
derive a crafting target's prerequisite chain deterministically; the release
contains such a chain resolver on its runtime repair path.  We study
demonstrations for three reasons.  The graph covers only part of the task:
\texttt{minecraft-data} lists crafting recipes but no smelting route (nothing
yields an iron ingot from raw iron) and no tool-tier ordering, and SAER's ingredient term is zero on 18 of the 60
tasks, every gathering and combat task among them (\secref{sec:scoring}).  A demonstration delivers the prerequisites already in
order and in the bot's executable command grammar.  And the agent we build on
receives tasks as free-form chat across categories, for which demonstrations
are the interface it already has.  The LLM is therefore our object of study,
not a competitor to a classical planner.  Supplying each target's resolved chain
is the natural next baseline, and we have not run it.

\subsection{Category Filtering}
\label{sec:catfilter}

The intent classifier (\texttt{getIntent()} in the release) asks the planning
LLM for one label from a fixed set: \texttt{craft}, \texttt{collect},
\texttt{cook}, \texttt{build}, \texttt{combat} and \texttt{explore}, plus a
\texttt{general} fallback.  \texttt{combat} was added \emph{after} the
evaluation runs, when we found the original classifier could not route to the
combat file (\appref{app:intent_oracle}).  Only demonstrations from the file
mapped to the label are admitted as retrieval candidates.  The filter is
motivated by the finding that irrelevant context degrades
reasoning~\citep{shi2023large}, not measured against it: no condition here runs unfiltered.  In the static
evaluation the category is the task's ground-truth label, so retrieval quality
is an \emph{oracle-category upper bound}; the bound is tight here, since the
deployed classifier routes 180/180 trials to the correct file, on one provider
endpoint and 60 templated prompts (\appref{app:intent_oracle}).

\subsection{Composite Scoring}
\label{sec:scoring}

Given a query task $q$ and a candidate example $e$ from the same
category, SAER computes a scalar relevance score $S(q, e) \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
S(q, e) = \alpha \cdot I(q,e) + \beta \cdot K(q,e) + \gamma \cdot M(q,e) + \delta \cdot L(q,e),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha{+}\beta{+}\gamma{+}\delta{=}1$.  The benchmark uses the
\emph{cold-start} weights $(0.50, 0.35, 0.00, 0.15)$ throughout, for an empty
inventory; a \emph{default} $(0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10)$ for a known inventory
targets mid-task replanning and is not evaluated here.  Cold start sets
$\gamma{=}0$ because $M$ never depends on $q$, so it would act as a standing
preference for low-tier examples rather than a relevance term.  The default's
4:3:2:1 ordering encodes our prior on each dimension's discriminative value
(ingredient overlap first, level only a tiebreaker).  Weights were
fixed before evaluation and no experiment here shows them optimal: on the
frozen benchmark the deployed weights are indistinguishable from uniform
(\appref{app:deployed_ablation}), and an earlier 35-task ablation is silent on
their ordering (\appref{app:weights}).  They are a working point, not a
demonstrated optimum.

All four components take values in $[0,1]$; the first two are Jaccard
overlaps, the last two normalised proximities.
\textbf{Ingredient Overlap ($I$).}  Let $R_q, R_e$ be the crafting
ingredient sets of $q$ and $e$, resolved from the \texttt{minecraft-data}
recipe graph: $I(q,e) = |R_q \cap R_e| / (|R_q \cup R_e| + \varepsilon)$,
$\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$; $I$ is identically zero when either side resolves to no
recipe, which measurement puts at all 16 gathering and combat tasks plus 2 of
the 44 crafting ones; on those 18 the ranking falls to $K$ and $L$ alone.
SAER's increment over BM25 is concentrated in the complement, where
$\alpha{\cdot}I$ can fire (\appref{app:strata}).
\textbf{Keyword Overlap ($K$).}  Let $W_q, W_e$ be content-word sets
(nouns, verbs, item names after stop-word removal):
$K(q,e) = |W_q \cap W_e| / (|W_q \cup W_e| + \varepsilon)$.
\textbf{Mining-Tier Gap ($M$).}  With $t_{\text{req}}(e) \in \{0,1,2,3\}$
the minimum pickaxe tier $e$ requires (0=none, 1=wooden, 2=stone, 3=iron)
and $t_{\text{player}}$ the agent's best tier,
$M = 1 - \max(0,\, t_{\text{req}}(e) - t_{\text{player}})/3$; with
$\gamma{=}0$ it enters no reported number.
\textbf{Level Proximity ($L$).}  Examples carry a hand-annotated
$\ell_e \in \{1,2,3\}$; the query's $\ell_q \in \{1,2,3,4\}$ comes from
surface keywords in the request (default 2), and
$L = 1 - |\ell_q - \ell_e|/3$.  The heuristic never reads the benchmark's tier
label and does not recover it --- it agrees on 20/60 tasks, what a constant
``L2'' guess scores --- so $\delta{\cdot}L$ carries no tier oracle
(\appref{app:intent_oracle}).
How the ranked examples enter the prompt, and why no term in that
construction is learned, is stated in \appref{app:prompt}.

\subsection{Context Assembly}
\label{sec:context}

The top-$k$ candidates ($k{=}3$) are ordered by descending $S(q,e)$,
so the most relevant comes first; the block itself sits mid-prompt,
after the command reference.  Each example is rendered as plain
text, not JSON: \texttt{Step N:}, \texttt{Prerequisites:},
\texttt{Produces:}, then one \texttt{Command:} per bot call; the model is
asked for the same shape with \texttt{Reasoning:} replacing
\texttt{Produces:} --- a step-by-step form after \citet{wei2022chain} and
ReAct~\citep{yao2023react}.

\subsection{Plan Validation}
\label{sec:planval}

The plan validator (\texttt{PlanValidator} in the release) checks a plan's
prerequisite structure over a virtual inventory before any in-game action.
At runtime, on failure, it permits one gap-repair regeneration before
execution (\appref{app:pseudocode}).  In the static study its walk is
reimplemented single-pass and repair-free as the scorer
(\secref{sec:protocol}), so the repair path is exercised only by the live study
(\appref{sec:live}), and every static number, Table~\ref{tab:main} and the
ablation included, measures the unrepaired plan.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}

We evaluate on a held-out benchmark of \textbf{60 tasks} spanning three
Minecraft task categories (44 crafting, 11 gathering, 5 combat) and
three difficulty tiers.  No task appears in the demonstration set, verified
by slug-level non-overlap checking after an earlier benchmark revision replaced
24 overlapping tasks.  The benchmark and training data were frozen before any
model evaluation began, and no task was added or removed after seeing model
outputs.  Residual structural overlap is modest.  For the 44
crafting tasks the symmetric ingredient Jaccard to the nearest demonstration
has mean 0.188 (0.290 on a later corrected bank) and never exceeds 0.333 on
the bank the runs retrieved over; no gathering task's goal item is the final
product of any demonstration, whereas the same check flags all five combat
tasks (\secref{sec:combat_design}).  On the evaluation-time bank the gain
rises with overlap, but the direction reverses on the corrected bank, and the
high-overlap stratum is the iron- and stone-tier multi-hop chains, so overlap
is substantially a proxy for prerequisite depth and neither panel points to
near-copies (\appref{app:overlap}).

\textbf{L1} (20 tasks): single-material tasks achievable with basic
tools (\texttt{wooden\_sword}, \texttt{chest}, \texttt{bowl}).
\textbf{L2} (20 tasks): two-chain tasks typically culminating
in iron-tool use (\texttt{iron\_axe}, \texttt{cauldron},
\texttt{hopper}); L2 is the primary discrimination tier because it
requires the complete wooden$\to$stone$\to$iron pickaxe chain.
\textbf{L3} (20 tasks): three-or-more-chain tasks involving deep
dependency trees (\texttt{compass}, \texttt{golden\_pickaxe},
\texttt{anvil}).

\subsection{Models and Conditions}
\label{sec:models}

We evaluate seven models from five organisations (\appref{app:models}): five
primary-study models under \texttt{no\_retrieval} and \texttt{saer\_cold},
and a two-model open-weight replication panel (Gemma4-26B/31B) outside the
pre-specified test family.  Four retrieval conditions are compared on
Qwen2.5-Max, the ablation model.  Sampling temperature is 0.7 for Qwen3-8B,
GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet~4.5, the value in the harness's evaluation profiles,
not tuned on this benchmark.  The Qwen2.5-Max, DeepSeek-V3 and Gemma4 requests
set no temperature, so those endpoints used their server defaults, which the
run files do not record (\appref{app:models}).  Every within-model
comparison, and so every retrieval effect, keeps the model and its setting
fixed; levels across models, and the three cross-model tests of the family,
mix settings.

\noindent\textbf{Conditions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{no\_retrieval}: No retrieved examples; the task and the shared rule text only
  (\appref{app:prompt}).
  \item \textbf{saer\_cold}: SAER with cold-start weights
  $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta){=}(0.50,0.35,0.00,0.15)$; agent
  inventory treated as empty.
  \item \textbf{random\_retrieval} (Qwen2.5-Max ablation only): Three
  examples selected uniformly at random from the same-category training
  set.
  \item \textbf{bm25\_retrieval} (Qwen2.5-Max ablation \emph{and}
  evaluated cross-model on all five primary-study models, \secref{sec:ablation}): Okapi
  BM25~\citep{robertson2009probabilistic} ($k_1{=}1.5$, $b_L{=}0.75$;
  $b_L$ is BM25's length-normalisation term, not McNemar's $b$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textbf{Static plan evaluation.}  For each task and condition the planning
LLM writes a plan, which is scored offline without a live bot: the validator
walks the plan's commands (\texttt{collectBlocks}, \texttt{craftRecipe},
\texttt{smeltItem}) over a virtual inventory that starts empty (pseudocode in
\appref{app:pseudocode}).  Each condition is run with three seeds; a task
passes if it passes in at least two (\textbf{majority vote}).

\textbf{Metrics.}  The primary metric is the \emph{Strict Plan Pass Rate}
(SPR; labelled TCR, for task completion rate, in the released scripts\footnote{Code and data: \url{https://github.com/Jayden23018/existence-before-relevance}.}): the fraction of tasks whose plan
has no prerequisite gap in the inventory walk and ends with the goal item
present.  SPR measures the prerequisite-structure validity of a plan, not task
completion.  \emph{Plan Validity Rate} (PVR) checks only \emph{explicitly
declared} preconditions and the goal item, not implicit tool-tier
requirements, so the gap PVR$-$SPR separates structural failures from
detail-level ones.

\textbf{Two scorings.}  The pre-specified scorer credits each produced item
but never debits consumed ones, on the rationale that quantities are the
executor's job; an item produced once satisfies every later requirement.  We
therefore also report a \emph{debited} SPR, which charges each craft and smelt
so that every produced unit is spent once (\appref{app:exposure}).  The
leniency is not symmetric: on Qwen2.5-Max, debiting removes 0 of
\texttt{no\_retrieval}'s 19 passes but 7 of random's 38, 14 of BM25's 42 and
16 of SAER's 49, because a scorer that never debits can only over-credit plans
that produce things, and retrieval makes plans produce more.  Credit-only
remains the confirmatory scorer, since the test family was fixed for it;
debited figures appear beside it in Tables~\ref{tab:main}
and~\ref{tab:ablation}.  Against the published Plancraft oracle the scorer
agrees on 95.8\% of 11,177 craft invocations ($\kappa{=}0.753$).  That
certifies single-craft feasibility only: one issue class of six, not the
tool-gap class behind two models' failures (\appref{app:plancraft}).

\textbf{Re-validation.}  Two harness defects were found after generation: a
missing recipe-resolver hook that disabled three of SAER's scoring dimensions,
and a missing mining-drop alias in the scorer.  Every \texttt{saer\_cold} arm in Table~\ref{tab:main}
was regenerated after the fix, so each is a later run than its
\texttt{no\_retrieval} arm, and every stored seed plan of every condition was
then re-scored with one fixed validator.  All confirmatory SPR, PVR and
McNemar values come from that pass (\appref{app:provenance}); three
exploratory panels quote run-time verdicts beside re-scored ones
(\appref{app:livecombat}, \appref{app:capacity}, \appref{app:thinking}).

\textbf{Statistical testing.}  McNemar's exact binomial
test~\citep{mcnemar1947note} on per-task majority-vote outcomes,
$\alpha{=}0.05$, Bonferroni-corrected over 13 pre-specified tests (5
per-model $+$ 5 ablation $+$ 3 cross-model): $\alpha'{=}0.0038$.  We call a
result \emph{family-wise significant} if $p{<}\alpha'$, \emph{nominal} if
$0.0038{\le}p{<}0.05$, and \emph{not significant} otherwise; every headline
verdict is a Bonferroni verdict.  The uniformly more powerful Holm
step-down~\citep{holm1979simple} over the same 13 tests is reported post hoc
as a labelled sensitivity analysis (Holm rejects 11, Bonferroni 9;
\appref{app:mcnemar}).  The Gemma4 panel lies outside the family and is
reported descriptively.  The 55 demonstrations (25 crafting, 21
collecting, 9 combat) were AI-drafted from hand-written seeds and hand-checked
(\appref{app:traindata}).

\subsection{Combat Subgroup}
\label{sec:combat_design}

The five combat tasks (L1--L3) are part of the 60 and counted in
Table~\ref{tab:main}, so they form a within-benchmark subgroup, not a
hold-out.  Five of the nine combat demonstrations target the same five mobs
and differ only by slug prefix (\texttt{combat/creeper} vs.\
\texttt{combat/kill\_creeper}), which the slug-level check misses; the runs
behind Table~\ref{tab:main} retrieve over them (\appref{app:nocombat} bounds

all four conditions without those five demonstrations, over a combat pool with acquisition chains
corrected for a cold start (\appref{app:livecombat}).

\noindent\textbf{Finding~1: an L2 bottleneck in five of seven models.}  The
five primary-study models score 10--30\% on L2 without retrieval (median
15\%; a 2$\times$5 homogeneity test does not reject, $p{=}0.54$) against
45--95\% on L1 (median 85\%).  The Gemma4 replication panel already clears
60--65\% on L2 without retrieval, so the diagnosis applies to the five primary
models, not to every model we ran.  We have no tested explanation for the difference.

\noindent\textbf{Finding~2: retrieval helps all five, the 8B model
included.}  SAER improves all five primary models by $+$20 to $+$50\,pp, four
family-wise significantly; Qwen3-8B gains least ($+$20\,pp, $p{=}0.0075$:
Holm yes, Bonferroni no).  Under debiting all five gains stay positive
($+$16 to $+$24\,pp) and three clear $\alpha'$.  Gain does not track parameter
count (DeepSeek-V3, 671\,B, gains less than Qwen2.5-Max), and two of five sizes are undisclosed and a third only approximate
(\appref{app:models}), so we make no capacity-scaling claim.  Qwen3-8B keeps
the largest PVR$-$SPR gap ($+$22 vs.\ $+3$--$+7$): given correct templates it

distinct from the bottleneck.  Of the five, DeepSeek-V3 alone reaches 10/20 at L3 without
retrieval (others 0--3/20); with SAER it converges with Qwen2.5-Max
overall ($p{=}0.453$).  On the replication panel the credit-only gains
($+$7 and $+$11\,pp) do not survive debiting.

non-monotone in parameter count.  On Qwen2.5-Max random retrieval alone lifts
L2 from 10\% to 70\%, and \texttt{saer\_cold} reaches the same 14/20, so L2
does not separate SAER from random; BM25 trails both there (11/20) but leads
random at L3 (14/20 vs.\ 7/20).  As BM25 and random do not differ overall
($p{=}0.424$), we read neither tier split as more than noise (per-tier counts
in \appref{app:perseed}).

($+$31\,pp; $+$20\,pp debited, $b{=}13$, $c{=}1$, $p{=}0.0018$), family-wise

smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is $2\cdot0.5^{7}{=}0.0156$, so the

before its SAER arm on a provider alias, not a date-pinned identifier, so an
endpoint change is a rival explanation that re-scoring cannot remove
(\appref{app:provenance}).  \emph{Replication:} across the five primary models
SAER beats BM25 on four (nominally on two) and trails by one task on GPT-4o;
the one-sided 4--1 sign test gives $p{=}0.188$, and six of the seven discordant tasks lie in the 42
where ingredient overlap is non-zero (\appref{app:strata}).  One check
cuts the other way: SAER is handed the \emph{more} defective demonstrations
(top-1 materially short on 22/60 tasks vs.\ BM25's 8/60, $p{=}0.00012$) and
still scores higher, so demonstration quality does not explain its edge
(\appref{app:exposure}).  We read relevance as a consistent but unconfirmed
increment.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection{Why L2 Fails, and What Demonstrations Transmit}
\label{sec:knowledge_gap}

The L2 wall holds with the tool chain stated in the rule text, and it does not
track scale.  Counting the
scorer's typed issues over every no-retrieval failure
(\appref{app:failmodes}), 20--41 of each primary model's 27--46 failing tasks
(74--95\%) carry an \emph{unacquired prerequisite}: a declared precondition, a
craft ingredient or a tool never obtained.  The signature is shared, its
composition is not: ingredient gaps run 39 tasks on Qwen3-8B against 3 on
GPT-4o, and tool gaps 33 on Qwen2.5-Max against 2 on Claude Sonnet~4.5, so we
claim no common failure profile, and the version-specific naming check fires
on no failure in the primary study (the rule text states the drop names).  Correctness binds on one exploratory
contrast: on a superseded 35-task benchmark six L2 demonstrations declared a
stone pickaxe they never crafted, and under one scorer SAER read 1/13 at L2
against random's 6/13; the post-repair reading (11--12/13) is confounded with
two concurrent validator fixes, and the episode predates every run reported
here (\appref{app:weights}).

\textbf{Defects are inherited verbatim.}  The mechanism behind that contrast
is measurable on the frozen runs.  All nine combat demonstrations use
\texttt{!equipItem}, which is not in the bot's command registry
(\texttt{!equip} is).  Across every stored combat-task plan, superseded runs
included, the string appears in 0 of 505 trials without retrieval and in 726
of 1,016 with it (71.5\%; 72.9\% under SAER, 74.2\% BM25, 61.2\% random): the
models never invent the command and copy it about three times in four once
shown it (\appref{app:intent_oracle}).  Quantities travel the same way.  Nine
of the collecting bank's 17 \texttt{oak\_planks} calls ask for 4, too few for
a crafting table, sticks and a pickaxe.  On the 11 gathering tasks four of the
five models never emit 4 without retrieval (the rule text's chain shows 12), yet under SAER they do in 10 of 12
trials on Qwen2.5-Max, 4 of 15 on DeepSeek-V3 and 2 of 17 on GPT-4o, while
Claude Sonnet~4.5 overrides the demonstration every time (Qwen3-8B emits 4
under both conditions; \appref{app:quantity}).  Neither defect moves a
credit-only number, since that scorer credits no equip step and charges no
quantity, which is why both survived to be found.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\noindent\textbf{Scope.}  Beyond the offline scope stated in \secref{sec:intro}, the live study (126 trials on DeepSeek-V3) floors both conditions at 2/21 L2
trials because execution-framework failures dominate, so it neither
corroborates nor contradicts the static result (\appref{sec:live}).  The
cross-agent study (\appref{app:crossagent}) probes a different retrieval stage
and carries no claim.  \textbf{Metric.}  Credit-only SPR over-credits the retrieval arms more than
the baseline (\secref{sec:protocol}); scoring is automated and unblinded; and
McNemar on majority-vote outcomes models neither task difficulty nor
model$\times$condition interaction.

\noindent\textbf{Relevance evidence.}  One pre-specified test on one model, seven
discordant tasks and a 16-day generation gap on a provider alias; settling it
needs a same-window rerun, which we have not done.  The category filter is in
every retrieval condition, so its own contribution is unmeasured.  \textbf{Controls not run.}  We ablate retrieval \emph{policy}, not demonstration
\emph{surface form}: format-only and content-masked controls, and supplying each target's resolved chain, are future work.

\noindent\textbf{Models and decoding.}  Temperature differs across models
(\secref{sec:models}); two primary models used date-pinned identifiers, and the
other three are provider aliases, so endpoint updates between their arms cannot
be excluded.  Three further 8--12\,B models, run at provider
defaults, keep SAER ceilings at 7/26/34 of 60 when re-scored (7/23/33 at run time), below the four frontier primary
models (41--52/60) but not all below Qwen3-8B's 26/60, so we claim no
capacity threshold (\appref{app:capacity}).  Without \texttt{minecraft-data} SAER keeps only keyword and level, as on
18/60 tasks already (\appref{app:strata}).

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Five of the seven LLM planners we tested pass only 10--30\% of two-chain tasks
without retrieval; in 74--95\% of those failures a prerequisite is never acquired
(\secref{sec:knowledge_gap}).  Given a shared rule text, the primary lever is the \emph{existence} of a
same-category demonstration ($+$31\,pp on Qwen2.5-Max; $+$20\,pp debited).
\emph{Relevance} adds an increment that neither its discordance budget nor the
stricter scorer confirms, concentrated in the 42 tasks with ingredient overlap;
on \emph{capacity}, the 8B model gains too.  Demonstrations are inherited, errors
included, so their \emph{correctness} is the next lever, a natural target for
verifiable-reward RL~\citep{zeng2025rlve}.

\section*{How to Read These Appendices}

\paragraph{No parameter updates.}  Every condition reported anywhere in
this paper is inference-time only: the model endpoint and its weights
are fixed, and only the input prompt is rebuilt
(\appref{app:prompt}).  The files named \texttt{*\_examples.json} under
\texttt{data/training/} in the code release are \emph{frozen
demonstrations used as prompt data}, not examples used to train or
fine-tune any evaluated model.  No optimiser, gradient update,
fine-tuning pass or weight persistence occurs at any point in the
pipeline.

\paragraph{Confirmatory versus exploratory.}  The confirmatory evidence
base is the frozen 60-task benchmark evaluated under the fixed static
evaluator of \secref{sec:protocol}, with the pre-specified 13-test
Bonferroni family of \appref{sec:stats} ($\alpha'{=}0.0038$).  Several
appendices below instead report an \emph{earlier} benchmark version (35
or 42 tasks), a \emph{mismatched} weight configuration, \emph{additional}
sub-frontier models outside the pre-specified family, or an evaluator version predating the
\texttt{setRecipeResolver} fix.  Each of those carries
\textbf{Exploratory} in its heading and its own caveat in its opening
line.  They are retained for transparency and to motivate future
ablations; \emph{no claim in the main text rests on them}, and they are
never pooled with the confirmatory numbers.  Where an appendix figure
disagrees with a main-text figure, the main text governs, and
\appref{app:provenance} names the run that produced it.

\paragraph{The last appendix carries no claim.}  \appref{sec:multiagent}
reports a two-bot live study of a second retrieval stage of the same enhancer.
It is kept so that the released system can be read end to end, exercises none
of the components the main study varies, and can be skipped by a reader
auditing the paper's claims.

\label{app:plancraft}
\noindent\textbf{Cross-validation against Plancraft oracle.}
We cross-validate the ingredient-feasibility check against the
independent, published Plancraft oracle planner~\citep{dagan2024plancraft}.
The comparison is pinned to the 16 runs behind the primary-study rows of
Table~\ref{tab:main}, all of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and the per-model BM25
rows of Table~\ref{tab:bm25_permodel} --- five models
$\times$ \{\texttt{no\_retrieval}, \texttt{bm25}, \texttt{saer\_cold}\}, plus
Qwen2.5-Max's \texttt{random} arm, 48 seed files.  The Gemma4 replication
runs are not included.  Across their 11,177
\texttt{craftRecipe} invocations the two validators agree on
\textbf{95.8\%} (10,711/11,177; Cohen's
$\kappa{=}0.753$~\citep{landis1977measurement}), with 427
validator-lenient and 39 validator-strict disagreements.  Selection is by
run identity, not by file date: an earlier revision of this script took
``the most recent seed files'' from the results directory, which never
looked inside the two subdirectories holding the primary runs and
re-targeted itself every time a later ablation was added.  The figure above
is the pinned one, reproducible with
\texttt{node scripts/eval/plancraft\_crossval.mjs}.  The oracle is the
published \texttt{plancraft} package (v0.3.34) called through a local batch
shim, not a reimplementation of it, so the comparison is against the authors'
own planner rather than our reading of it.

\noindent\textbf{Per-condition false positives.}
Table~\ref{tab:fpr} splits the disagreements by condition.  Homogeneity is
not rejected ($\chi^2(3){=}4.78$, $p{=}0.19$, smallest expected count 27.7),
and the per-invocation ordering --- most lenient on \texttt{no\_retrieval}
(4.47\%), least on \texttt{saer\_cold} (3.46\%) --- does \emph{not} license the
reading that residual leniency favours the baseline arm.  Strict-pass is a
plan-level predicate: one leniently credited craft can carry a whole plan, so
the quantity that matters is lenient credits \emph{per plan}, and the arms
differ in how many crafts a plan contains.  Across the 2{,}880 plans in these
48 files the retrieval arms issue about half again as many craft calls each
(4.0--4.5 against \texttt{no\_retrieval}'s 2.9), and the two effects multiply
out the other way: 0.130 leniently credited crafts per plan on
\texttt{no\_retrieval} against 0.154 on \texttt{saer\_cold}, 0.157 on
\texttt{bm25} and 0.167 on \texttt{random}.  We therefore claim no direction
from this table.  It bounds recipe-level disagreement; whether scorer leniency
can account for the reported margins is answered instead by
\appref{app:exposure}, which charges the entire material budget --- a far
larger change than repairing these disagreements --- and finds the existence
effect and all five per-model effects still positive.  The scorer operates on deterministic game data, independent of SAER's
scoring function.

\noindent\textbf{What this agreement does and does not cover.}  The
cross-validation walks each plan accumulating a virtual inventory that,
like the scorer's, is credited but never debited, and puts a
\emph{per-invocation} question to the oracle: given this inventory
state, is this one craft solvable?  Both sides therefore receive the
same non-consuming state, so the procedure cannot by construction detect
a whole-plan double-spend --- the disagreements it can find are
recipe-level, not budget-level.  The 95.8\% should accordingly be read
as ``the two agree on single-craft feasibility given a state'', not as
``the scored plans are materially executable''.  It also covers one of the
scorer's six issue classes.  The other five --- tool, smelt, precondition,
naming and goal --- have no external validation here, and the tool class is
the one that dominates the failures of GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-Max
(Table~\ref{tab:failmodes}).  The aggregate material
budget is a separate question, which the scorer deliberately does not
enforce (\appref{app:validator}) and which
\appref{app:demoquality} takes up for the demonstration bank.

\section{SAER Prompt Assembly and the No-Update Property}
\label{app:prompt}

The four component scores are defined in \secref{sec:scoring}
(ingredient overlap $I$, keyword overlap $K$, mining-tier gap $M$, level
proximity $L$) and are not repeated here.  What the score is \emph{used
for} is stated here, because that is both the point at which the
symbolic retriever hands off to the neural planner and the point at
which the claim ``no parameter updates'' becomes checkable.

Let $q$ be the request, $x$ the agent state,
$c{=}\texttt{getIntent}(q)$ the inferred category and $D_c$ the
corresponding demonstration set.  After scoring, the enhancer builds the
base prompt
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{base}}(q,x)=\operatorname{concat}\bigl(R,\;
\operatorname{Top}_k(D_c;S),\; q\bigr),
\label{eq:prompt}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the command and rule documentation,
$\operatorname{Top}_k$ supplies the $k{=}3$ highest-ranked
demonstrations, and the unchanged model returns
$\hat y_0 = M_\theta(P_{\mathrm{base}})$ with
$\theta_{\mathrm{after}}=\theta_{\mathrm{before}}$.  Selected examples
are serialised in descending score order, so the strongest match comes
first within the demonstration block, which itself follows the command
reference and precedes the task statement.

The text $R$ is the same for every category and condition, and it is not
bare.  It lists the tool recipe patterns, the pickaxe tier each ore needs, a
generic cold-start tool chain that ends at mining iron ore, the raw-ore drop names and
the output format.  So \texttt{no\_retrieval} measures planning from these
rules with no retrieved demonstration, not a model given only the task.

Every condition in this paper differs only in which $D_c$ and which
ranking populate $\operatorname{Top}_k$: \texttt{no\_retrieval} sets it
empty, \texttt{random\_retrieval} draws uniformly, \texttt{bm25} ranks
lexically, and \texttt{saer\_cold} ranks by $S$.  Nothing else in
Equation~\ref{eq:prompt} varies, and no term in it is learned.

\section{Execution Modules}
\label{sec:exec}

The validated step array is executed by \textbf{TaskManager}, which
maintains a last-in-first-out (LIFO) task stack with three-layer error recovery (transient
failures, missing prerequisites, semantic errors).
\textbf{BlockNameResolver} canonicalises fuzzy block names to official
Minecraft~1.20.4 identifiers via environment scan,
\texttt{minecraft-data} lookup, and hardcoded fallback.  These modules
operate at runtime only and are not exercised by the static evaluation
in \secref{sec:protocol}.

\section{Training Data}

The demonstration pool consists of \textbf{25 crafting examples},

\section{Denominator Sensitivity}
\label{app:denom}
One benchmark task, \texttt{craft/iron\_horse\_armor} (L3), has no
crafting recipe in vanilla Minecraft 1.20.4 (\texttt{mcData} lists zero
recipes for this item; it is loot/trade-only) and no loot or trade
command exists in the bot's action space, so it is structurally
infeasible under our protocol regardless of model or retrieval
condition. We verified this directly rather than by assumption:
re-running the validator over the two canonical result directories
(102 occurrences of this task across all models, conditions, and
seeds) confirms \texttt{strict\_pass} and \texttt{pvr} are
\texttt{false} in \emph{all} 102 --- a fully concordant failure.
Widening the scan to every seed file in the repository, including
superseded runs, yields 225 occurrences and likewise zero passes under
either metric.
Because a task that fails identically in both arms of every paired
comparison contributes to neither McNemar's $b$ nor $c$, its inclusion
cannot affect any paired significance test; it only depresses every
condition's absolute pass rate by a constant one task.

Table~\ref{tab:denom-sensitivity} reports Table~\ref{tab:main} and
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_5cond} SPR values recomputed with

identical in both columns for every row --- the task never counts as a
pass under either denominator, so this changes only the percentage,
not the pass count. Every McNemar $b/c/p$ in the main text and in
Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_appendix} below is unchanged; reported SPR percentages shift by at most $+1$--$2$\,pp and
no significance conclusion changes in either direction. We report the
pre-specified /60 result as primary throughout the paper and treat this
table as a sensitivity check confirming that result is not an artefact
of the one infeasible task.

\section{Statistical Significance}
\label{sec:stats}

The per-model McNemar exact tests (\appref{app:mcnemar};
$b$/$c$ = SAER-only / no\_ret-only passes) ground the
Main Results claims: SAER's per-model effect is Bonferroni-significant
($\alpha'{=}0.0038$) for four of the five primary-study models, and
nominally significant for the remaining one (Qwen3-8B, $p{=}0.0075$).
Under the post hoc Holm step-down over the same 13-test family
(\appref{app:mcnemar}) all five per-model effects are family-wise
significant; Bonferroni remains the pre-specified primary analysis.
All five primary-study paired-difference 95\% confidence intervals
(method for matched proportions of \citet{newcombe1998improved}) exclude zero, from
$[{+}7,{+}32]$\,pp (Qwen3-8B) to $[{+}36,{+}61]$\,pp (Qwen2.5-Max).  The
Gemma4 replication panel is reported under both SAER arms.  Under
warm-start (\texttt{saer\_full}, generated before the recipe-resolver fix) the arms read
48/60 SPR with 57/60 PVR for Gemma4-26B and 51/60 with 58/60 for Gemma4-31B,
against the same \texttt{no\_retrieval} baselines, giving $b{=}5$, $c{=}1$,
$p{=}0.22$ and $b{=}5$, $c{=}0$, $p{=}0.06$; under the primary study's cold-start protocol
--- same \texttt{saer\_cold} batch and the same validator, though the panel's
\texttt{no\_retrieval} arm was generated 6--8 days after the primary study's
(\appref{app:provenance}) --- they are $+$7\,pp ($b{=}8$, $c{=}4$, $p{=}0.39$) for Gemma4-26B and $+$11\,pp
($b{=}7$, $c{=}0$, $p{=}0.0156$) for Gemma4-31B.  All four lie outside the
13-test family and are uncorrected; the panel is excluded from the family
because it lies outside the pre-specified grid, not because its cold-start arm
is less comparable.  Under the debiting scorer both cold-start gains vanish
(Gemma4-26B 35$\to$28, $p{=}0.118$; Gemma4-31B 37$\to$37;
Table~\ref{tab:debit_permodel}).  The more informative fact about this
panel is that both models already score above the L2 wall without
retrieval, leaving little for retrieval to recover.
The 13-test family (composition in \secref{sec:protocol}) is the study's fixed evaluation
grid, set before results were examined and reported in full regardless of
outcome: no comparison was added or dropped based on significance.  The
Holm analysis re-thresholds that same fixed grid --- it adds no test and
removes none --- but the decision to run it was made after the results
were seen, so it is labelled post hoc throughout.



The ablation and cross-model panels (\appref{app:mcnemar}) add that all retrieval conditions---including random---significantly
beat no-retrieval for Qwen2.5-Max (all $p{<}0.001$; SAER-vs-BM25 there
examined in \secref{sec:ablation}), Qwen2.5-Max and DeepSeek-V3 with SAER significantly
outperform Qwen3-8B with SAER (the only two cross-model tests against it), and
DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen2.5-Max converge ($b{=}5$, $c{=}2$,
$p{=}0.453$, n.s.).

\section{Live Execution: a Framework-Bound Axis}
\label{sec:live}

To test whether static plan quality transfers to embodied execution, we
ran 21 of the 60 benchmark tasks --- seven per tier, chosen for staging
tractability rather than by a pre-specified rule --- on a live
Minecraft~1.20.4 server under
DeepSeek-V3 in two conditions (no\_retrieval, saer\_cold) $\times$ three
seeds (126 trials; \appref{app:livecombat}), scoring \emph{strict}
completion (goal item obtained) and \emph{prerequisite-chain} completion
(direct ingredients assembled, terminal craft excluded).

\textbf{Live does not separate the conditions, and the reason is
diagnostic.}  The 21 tasks split seven per tier, so a per-tier denominator of
21 counts trials (7 tasks $\times$ 3 seeds) while \emph{21 tasks} counts
tasks.  At the L2 discrimination tier both conditions floor at
2/21 trials (10\%) under \emph{both} metrics, and 11 of 21 tasks fail under both
in all three seeds---a symmetry no knowledge-driven account predicts.  A
failure-mode audit places most failures outside the knowledge axis:
33/42 (79\%) of no\_retrieval failures are framework-execution errors
(Mineflayer sync races) against 2 prerequisite-chain gaps, and 21/46
saer\_cold failures trace to a since-fixed defect mis-marking bundled craft
steps as successful (\appref{app:livecombat}); since the prerequisite-chain metric
excludes the terminal craft yet the L2 floor holds, the failure sits
\emph{upstream}.  At L1 the two conditions are close (strict 15/21
vs.\ 18/21, tying at 18/21 on the prerequisite-chain metric), and SAER's
examples lengthen plans (5.4 vs.\ 4.3 steps).  Live completion is therefore
governed by an execution-reliability axis floored equally for both
conditions, leaving no headroom for a plan-quality difference: a scope
limitation, not evidence either way (details in \appref{app:livecombat}).

\section{Combat Subgroup: An Existence Probe}
\label{sec:combat_probe}

The combat probe (design \secref{sec:combat_design}; per-condition table in \appref{app:livecombat}) reproduces the
existence effect on the combat subgroup: \texttt{no\_retrieval} passes
0/5 while random, BM25 and \texttt{saer\_cold} each pass the same
3/5 tasks.  The split sits at L1--L2 (0/1 and 0/2 without retrieval
vs.\ 1/1 and 2/2 with it); on the run-time verdicts quoted here both L3
tasks fail under every condition, so the top tier has no headroom.
(Under the uniform re-scoring of \secref{sec:protocol},
\texttt{combat/kill\_blaze} passes under \texttt{saer\_cold}, which
reads 4/5 rather than 3/5; both sets are given in
\appref{app:livecombat}.)  It cannot rank the variants: the margin
is at most one task, and at $n{=}5$ even a perfect 5--0 discordant split gives
$p{=}0.0625$ two-sided.  These five tasks are members of the 60-task
benchmark and are already counted in every $/60$ cell of
Table~\ref{tab:main}; the probe re-runs them with the near-duplicate
templates removed, so it is a subgroup analysis of the primary study
and not an independent replication of it.  We therefore read combat as an underpowered existence
check only and draw the relevance conclusion solely from the
60-task ablation (\secref{sec:ablation}).

\section{Weight Configuration Ablation Detail (Exploratory, 35-Task Benchmark)}
\label{app:weights}

\noindent\textbf{Exploratory; not confirmatory for the deployed
scorer.}  This study used the original 35-task benchmark, Qwen-8B
(\texttt{qwen3-vl-8b-instruct}, a different model from the main study's
Qwen3-8B), and
an older \emph{four}-component formulation with 4:3:2:1 weights, in
which $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ corresponded to ingredient overlap,
keyword overlap, mining-tool compatibility and difficulty match.  The
frozen 60-task results are produced by the current cold-start scorer,
which is \emph{three}-component --- $(\alpha,\beta,\delta){=}(0.50,0.35,0.15)$ with
the mining-tier term at $\gamma{=}0$ under an empty inventory (\secref{sec:scoring}).
The configuration exercised here is therefore not the configuration
deployed in Table~\ref{tab:main}.  The historical result motivates
a new ablation of the deployed weights; it does not validate them, and
no main-text claim rests on it.  Table~\ref{tab:weights_ablation}
reports the configurations tried.

\paragraph{The template-correctness episode, on the same benchmark.}
\secref{sec:knowledge_gap} cites one contrast for the correctness facet, and it
comes from this benchmark rather than the frozen one.  Six L2 crafting
demonstrations (\texttt{iron\_axe}, \texttt{iron\_pickaxe},
\texttt{iron\_shovel}, \texttt{iron\_hoe}, \texttt{shears}, \texttt{shield})
declared \texttt{stone\_pickaxe:1} as a step-2 precondition while no earlier
step crafted a wooden or stone pickaxe, and the planners followed the template
verbatim.  Under the scorer of the time, \texttt{saer\_cold} read \textbf{1/13}
at L2 against \texttt{random\_retrieval}'s \textbf{6/13} --- the retriever that
selects the most structurally similar demonstration is the one that inherits
its defect, so a relevance-ranked retriever can fall \emph{below} a random one
when the bank is wrong.  That is the whole of what this episode licenses, and
three limits bound it.  It is a single run per arm with no paired test, so the
figures carry no interval.  After the repair \texttt{saer\_cold} read 11--12/13
across three runs, but two validator bugs were fixed in the same batch (a
\texttt{None (assume access:trees)} string parsed as an item requirement, and
\texttt{produces} inference running only when a step declared no outputs), so
the post-repair half cannot be attributed to the bank.  And the repair predates
the freeze of the 60-task benchmark and every run reported in this paper: the
earliest timestamp in Table~\ref{tab:provenance} is later, so no reported
number comes from the defective bank.  A controlled version of this contrast
--- the same frozen benchmark, defective and repaired banks crossed with SAER
and random --- is the experiment the correctness facet still lacks.

\paragraph{What this table does and does not corroborate.}  A weight vector
invites the reading that its \emph{ordering}
$\alpha{>}\beta{>}\gamma{>}\delta$ was established somewhere.  It was not, and
this table is the closest thing to a test of it, so we state precisely what it
supports.

run the other way --- keyword alone (36.2\%) scores above ingredient
alone (28.6\%), while the ordering places ingredient first.  Single
dimensions in isolation are a weak probe of a dimension's marginal
contribution inside a combination, so we do not read the reversal as
refuting the ordering either; the honest statement is that this table
is silent on the ordering, and no experiment in this paper measures it.
The weight ordering of \secref{sec:scoring} should therefore be read as the
design rationale it is, fixed before evaluation, not as an empirically
ranked result.

\emph{Not corroborated: the necessity of calibration.}  The $+$34\,pp
gap over equal weighting reads at first as confirming that a
calibrated rather than uniform combination is necessary.  It does not
support that reading, and we set it aside rather than carry it
forward.  \appref{app:deployed_ablation} re-runs
the comparison against the \emph{deployed} weights on the frozen
60-task benchmark, under the fixed validator, on two models, and finds
no detectable difference between calibrated and uniform weighting ---
with the direction of the (non-significant) difference inconsistent
across the two models.

\emph{What does survive.}  Combining the dimensions beats both
single-dimension rows in this table (73.3\% against 28.6\% and 36.2\%).
On the frozen benchmark we show only that the combination is not interchangeable
with any single dimension --- collapsing to one re-orders the selected
demonstrations for 48--58 of 60 tasks --- not that it scores higher there.  The deployed
weights are best described as one pre-specified working point among
several that perform comparably, and the 35-task gap above is retained
as a record of what the older four-component configuration did on the
older benchmark, not as evidence about the deployed scorer.

comparisons below are run on the frozen 60-task benchmark under the
fixed validator of \secref{sec:protocol}, but they were specified after the

\secref{sec:protocol}.  They are reported to address the question

\section{Structural-Overlap Robustness Check}
\label{app:overlap}
\label{app:overlap-robustness}

Exact target-name disjointness does not preclude structural
(ingredient/prerequisite) overlap, so we additionally computed, for
each of the 44 CRAFT-category held-out tasks, the ingredient-set
Jaccard similarity to its nearest demonstration (query set = the
task's declared final-craft materials; demonstration set = the union
of \texttt{items.inventory} preconditions across all of its plan
steps, i.e.\ its full prerequisite chain).  The nearest demonstration
is never a zero-overlap match (min$=$0.143, mean$=$0.386,
max$=$1.000, $n{=}44$); the 16 COLLECT/COMBAT tasks are excluded from
this specific metric because they have no declared final-craft
materials by task design (they collect raw blocks or kill mobs rather
than craft from ingredients), not because they are structurally
unrelated to the demonstration pool.

This measure is asymmetric---the query side uses only final-craft
materials while the demonstration side uses each example's full
prerequisite chain---which could overstate similarity. As a
robustness check we recomputed a symmetric variant, expanding the
query side to its own full prerequisite chain via the same static
dependency resolution used elsewhere in the pipeline (applied offline
from an empty inventory). The symmetric measure is lower and flatter
(min$=$0.000, mean$=$0.290, max$=$0.500, $n{=}44$) than the
asymmetric one, with no task reaching the near-duplicate range (0/44
above 0.75). At stricter thresholds, the asymmetric measure has 14/44
tasks at or above 0.5, 4/44 at or above 0.75, and 4/44 at or above 0.9
(i.e.\ 4 near-duplicate matches), whereas the symmetric measure has
only 3/44 at or above 0.5 and 0/44 at or above 0.75 or 0.9---confirming
that the apparent near-duplicates are an artefact of the asymmetric
comparison. The four asymmetric-measure exact matches (Jaccard$=$1.0:
\texttt{wooden\_sword}, \texttt{oak\_sign}, \texttt{wooden\_shovel},
\texttt{oak\_fence}, each matched to a wooden-tool demonstration)
arise because these tasks' declared material sets contain only two
items (a stick and a plank), so any other two-item wooden-tool set is
trivially identical---not because of retrieval-pool duplication; none
of the four survive under the symmetric measure.

\paragraph{Which bank revision.}  The crafting bank was revised after the
evaluation runs; 18 of its 25 examples differ between the two revisions, in
the edit classes enumerated in \appref{app:traindata}.  Both revisions ship in
the code and data release, and all three overlap scripts
(\texttt{jaccard\_overlap\_holdout\_vs\_demos.mjs},
\texttt{jaccard\_overlap\_symmetric.mjs},
\texttt{overlap\_tercile\_stratify.mjs}) take the bank path from the
\texttt{CRAFTING\_BANK} environment variable, so either block of
Table~\ref{tab:overlap-tercile} is reproducible.  The two questions this
section answers call for different revisions.  For the \emph{contamination
bound}, the corrected revision is the conservative choice, and the figures in
the two paragraphs above are computed on it: the two edits that touch ingredient sets ---
repairing the \texttt{crafting/gold\_shovel} prerequisite chain (3$\to$7
steps) and normalising one \texttt{planks}$\to$\texttt{oak\_planks}
precondition string --- both enlarge the demonstration side and therefore
\emph{raise} every measured overlap; the remaining edits change quantities
only, which this metric does not read.  Under the
evaluation-time revision --- the pool the reported runs actually
retrieved over --- overlap is uniformly lower: asymmetric min$=$0.167,
mean$=$0.317; symmetric mean$=$0.188, max$=$0.333, with
\textbf{0/44} tasks at or above 0.5 (versus 3/44), and \textbf{one}
exact asymmetric match rather than four.  We use the higher estimates for the
bound so that it is not flattered by the pool we ship.  For \emph{where the gain
occurs}, the evaluation-time revision is the relevant one, since it is the pool
the runs actually retrieved over; \secref{sec:tasks} quotes it, and it leads
Table~\ref{tab:overlap-tercile}.

We further stratified SAER's SPR gain on Qwen2.5-Max
(\texttt{no\_retrieval}$\to$\texttt{saer\_cold}) by overlap tercile
(low/mid/high, $n{=}15/15/14$) to test whether the gain is
concentrated in high-overlap, near-duplicate tasks---which would
undercut a generalisation claim.  On the evaluation-time bank the gain rises
monotonically with overlap ($+6/{+}8/{+}10$ asymmetric, $+5/{+}9/{+}10$
symmetric; Table~\ref{tab:overlap-tercile}, upper block).  This does not
support a near-duplicate explanation, for two reasons.  First, under that bank there are no near-duplicates for the
gain to concentrate in: the symmetric measure spans $0.000$--$0.333$
across all 44 tasks, so its ``high'' tercile ($0.231$--$0.333$) is a
low-overlap stratum in absolute terms, and a gradient across a range
that never reaches $0.5$ cannot be retrieval of a near-copy.  Second,
the tercile is confounded with difficulty by construction: that
stratum is seven \texttt{iron\_*} items, three \texttt{stone\_*}
items, \texttt{cauldron}, \texttt{minecart}, \texttt{compass} and
\texttt{lever}---the iron- and stone-tier multi-hop chains, which
share ingredients with the demonstrations and with each other simply
because those tiers have few distinct inputs.  Its no-retrieval floor
is \textbf{0/14}, the L2 bottleneck this paper is about, whereas the
corresponding low-overlap tercile starts from 7/15.  Under the corrected bank
the direction reverses: the gain is largest in the low- and mid-overlap
terciles (lower block), so the gradient is not stable across bank revisions,
and we draw no direction from either panel.  Overlap tercile is therefore
substantially a proxy for prerequisite depth; what flips between the two
panels is which end of that depth axis the terciles land on, not whether the
gain comes from retrieving near-copies.

gathering tasks against the 21 collecting demonstrations, no task's goal item
is the final product of any demonstration (0/11); one, \texttt{gravel},
appears as an intermediate collection step in two demonstrations
(\texttt{collect/flint}, \texttt{collect/blaze\_rod}).  The gathering tier
therefore holds no near-duplicate of the combat kind.

\paragraph{Holm step-down: a post hoc sensitivity analysis, not a
replacement.}  The Bonferroni correction above is the pre-specified
primary analysis, and every headline verdict in the main text is a
Bonferroni verdict.  Bonferroni is, however, conservative: it holds all
13 tests to the same threshold no matter how small the smallest
$p$-values are.  We therefore also report Holm's sequentially rejective
procedure~\citep{holm1979simple} over the identical 13 hypotheses.  Holm
controls the same family-wise error rate in the strong sense and is
uniformly more powerful, so relative to Bonferroni it can only add
rejections, never withdraw one.  We state plainly that this analysis was
added \emph{after} the results were known.  It is reported alongside the
pre-specified correction rather than in place of it, and it changes no
$p$-value, no effect size and no experimental number in this paper ---
only which of the two thresholding rules a given $p$ is being measured
against.  Choosing between them after the fact, and reporting only the
more favourable one, would not be a defensible analysis; both are given
wherever they disagree.

Holm sorts the 13 $p$-values ascending and compares the $i$-th against
$\alpha/(m{-}i{+}1)$ with $m{=}13$, stopping at the first
non-rejection and retaining every hypothesis from there on
(Table~\ref{tab:holm}).  Nine tests clear even the strictest, rank-1
threshold $\alpha/13{=}0.0038$ and are rejected under both procedures.
Holm additionally rejects two: the Qwen3-8B per-model test
($p{=}0.0075$ against $\alpha/4{=}0.0125$) and \texttt{saer\_cold} vs.\
BM25 ($p{=}0.0156$ against $\alpha/3{=}0.0167$).  The step-down then
halts at rank 12 ($p{=}0.424$ against $\alpha/2{=}0.025$), so both null
results remain null under either rule.  Holm thus rejects 11 of 13
where Bonferroni rejects 9 of 13, and the two affected claims are
flagged as such in \secref{sec:protocol}, \secref{sec:mainresults} and
\secref{sec:ablation}.

\emph{Robustness.}  The Holm verdict does not depend on the precise
values of the nine smallest $p$-values, only on the fact that they are
small.  Every one of them falls below the rank-1 threshold $0.0038$ ---
the largest is $6.6{\times}10^{-5}$ (DeepSeek-V3 per-model, $b{=}21$,
$c{=}2$), a margin of roughly $58\times$ --- so any permutation of their
internal ordering still satisfies all nine of the ranks-1--9
comparisons and leaves ranks 10--13 untouched.  The two Holm-only
rejections are the only verdicts in the family that turn on a $p$-value
close to its threshold ($0.0075$ vs.\ $0.0125$, and $0.0156$ vs.\
$0.0167$; the latter by a factor of $1.07$), and both are reported as
Holm-only wherever they appear.

\emph{Sensitivity to the family size.}  What the two Holm-only rejections
\emph{do} depend on is $m$, and we report that dependence rather than
leave it to be discovered.  Holding the observed $p$-values fixed and
enlarging the family: \texttt{saer\_cold} vs.\ BM25 ($p{=}0.0156$, rank

\section{Capacity De-aliasing with Additional Sub-frontier Models (Exploratory)}
\label{app:capacity}
\label{app:capacity_dealias}

\noindent\textbf{Exploratory; provider-default decoding.}  The three
models in this section are served through OpenRouter on a client path
that does not forward the \texttt{temperature} parameter, so they run
at provider defaults rather than the $T{=}0.7$ set for three of the five
primary models (footnote below; per-model settings in \appref{app:models}).  They are reported as a family-diversity check on the
capacity pattern, are not directly controlled against the main study,
and are excluded from the confirmatory Bonferroni family of \appref{sec:stats}.

\secref{sec:mainresults} makes no causal capacity claim, but it does rest
its smallest-model observation on a single data point, Qwen3-8B, which is
confounded with the Qwen3 generation: a small gain there could reflect
Qwen3-family template fragility rather than model scale.  To separate the
two, we
run three additional \emph{cross-family, non-Qwen} open-source models
of comparable scale under the identical 60-task, 3-seed protocol with
the \texttt{setRecipeResolver}-fixed static evaluator (\secref{sec:protocol}):
\textbf{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct}, \textbf{Ministral-8B-2512}, and
\textbf{Gemma-3-12B-it} (all served via OpenRouter).\footnote{The
two models named as future work in an earlier draft (Mistral-7B,
Gemma-2-9B) have been retired from our serving platform; we substitute
their in-family successors (Ministral-8B, Gemma-3-12B) at the same
parameter tier. The OpenRouter client path does not forward the
\texttt{temperature} parameter to the API (a known harness limitation
shared by all OpenRouter profiles); the models therefore run at their
provider default temperature.}  These span $\sim$8--12B parameters and
three independent families (Meta, Mistral, Google), so any shared
pattern cannot be attributed to the Qwen3 generation; Gemma-3-12B also
forms a same-family rung below the Gemma4-26B/31B models in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\textbf{Method: we compare SAER ceilings, not gains.}  The
no-retrieval floors of small models differ by up to 7$\times$
(Llama-3.1-8B 2/60 vs.\ Qwen3-8B 14/60), which inflates the
no\_ret$\to$saer\_cold gain in a way uninformative about capacity.
We therefore compare the \texttt{saer\_cold} SPR \emph{ceiling}: if the
three new models cluster with Qwen3-8B (26/60) \emph{below} the four frontier primary models (41--52/60), the residual is not specific to the Qwen3
generation.  We phrase the criterion this way deliberately.  An earlier
version read 41/60 as a \emph{capacity} floor; that reading does not
survive our own Table~\ref{tab:main}, where Gemma4-26B-A4B --- 4B active
parameters, fewer than any model in this panel --- reaches a cold-start
ceiling of 48/60, and Gemma4-31B reaches 53/60.  Within the Gemma family
the ordering inverts outright: Gemma-3-12B (12B dense) ceilings at
33/60 against 48/60 for a model with three times fewer active
parameters.  What this panel can de-alias is family, not capacity.  We additionally report \texttt{parse\_success}
(the fraction of tasks whose plan parsed into executable commands) to
confirm the ceiling reflects domain knowledge rather than
output-format compliance.  The five primary-study models are
format-compliant under no retrieval (parse-success $\approx$100\%, verified on
the canonical per-seed files), so for them the no-retrieval baseline is not a
format-compliance artefact.  Table~\ref{tab:capacity_dealias} gives the three models'
results.

confirmatory family of \appref{sec:stats} and its $p$-values are descriptive.

\label{app:livecombat}
\label{app:live}

Table~\ref{tab:live} reports the per-level live-execution pass rates
discussed in \appref{sec:live}.  Live completion is governed by an
execution-reliability axis (Mineflayer sync/window races; long embodied
mine--smelt--craft chains) that floors both conditions equally at the L2
tier, leaving no headroom for the plan-quality difference SAER supplies;
the figures are consistent with the bottleneck being severe but cannot
measure it, since both arms floor together; as \secref{sec:intro},
\appref{sec:live} and \secref{sec:limits} also state, this axis corroborates
nothing either way.  Unlike the static evaluations, this live study runs the
runtime \texttt{PlanValidator} with one gap-repair regeneration before
execution, applied identically to both conditions; since the L2 floor
is symmetric (2/21 under both \texttt{no\_retrieval} and
\texttt{saer\_cold}), we do not read this as directional, though
per-trial repair rates were not logged.

\paragraph{Failure-mode audit (\appref{sec:live}).}
Of the 42 \texttt{no\_retrieval} trial failures, 33 (79\%) carry an
explicit Mineflayer synchronisation error, 4 time out, 3 abort with no
item gained, and 2 are prerequisite-chain gaps.  The 46
\texttt{saer\_cold} failures are distributed differently: 25 are
prerequisite-chain gaps, and 21 of those report the same missing
\texttt{wooden\_pickaxe}.  We traced that cluster to a runtime defect
rather than a planning one.  A plan step whose command list bundles
several \texttt{craftRecipe} calls was judged successful whenever any
one of its outputs appeared in the inventory, so a final craft dropped by
a window-sync race was recorded as complete and never retried; the
missing tool then surfaced at the next mining step.  The defect is
triggered by the multi-command step format that SAER's demonstrations
induce and not by \texttt{no\_retrieval}'s one-command steps, which
give every craft its own verification point.  Splitting bundled steps to
one craft each removes the symptom in a three-task pilot
(\texttt{craft/iron\_door}, \texttt{craft/cauldron},
\texttt{craft/iron\_leggings} $\times$ 2 conditions $\times$ 3 seeds);
the fix is confined to the live runtime and does not touch the static
evaluation of Table~\ref{tab:main}.  A full re-run under the fix was not completed, so
Table~\ref{tab:live} reports the pre-fix results and we do not claim a
corrected live ranking---only that a substantial share of the
\texttt{saer\_cold} live deficit is an execution-layer artefact.  Plan-length
differences are not one-directional: \texttt{saer\_cold} plans are
longer at L1 (5.4 steps against 4.3 for \texttt{no\_retrieval}) but shorter
at L2 and L3 (9.7 and 12.2 steps against 12.6 and 16.5).

Table~\ref{tab:combat} gives the per-condition combat-domain results
(design \secref{sec:combat_design}; discussion \appref{sec:combat_probe}).  Two independent runs agree: retrieval
of any kind lifts majority-vote SPR from 0--1/5 to 3/5, and the
three retrieval conditions land on the \emph{identical} three tasks
(\texttt{kill\_creeper}, \texttt{kill\_enderman}, \texttt{kill\_slime});
\texttt{kill\_blaze} and \texttt{kill\_wither\_skeleton} fail under every
condition and every seed on the run-time verdicts quoted here; re-scoring moves
\texttt{kill\_blaze} (below).  That agreement is largely fixed by
construction: with the five near-duplicates excluded the pool holds four
demonstrations, so at $k{=}3$ any two retrieval conditions share at least two of their
three.  The probe therefore supports the
existence effect on the combat subgroup and supplies no evidence on retrieval
quality.  No paired test is reported because none can be informative
here: with 5 tasks, a perfect 5--0 discordant split gives $p{=}0.0625$
under the two-sided exact binomial used in the main text, above
$\alpha{=}0.05$ before the Bonferroni correction of \appref{sec:stats}, and the
observed splits are 3--0 at best.

demonstrations target the same five mobs as the held-out tasks and
differ only by slug prefix (\texttt{combat/creeper} vs.\

\secref{sec:tasks} does not flag them, and the Jaccard overlap check of \appref{app:overlap} covers only the 44 CRAFT
tasks.  Both runs below therefore
pass \texttt{-{}-exclude-slugs} to remove those five templates from the

originally assumed a warm inventory without acquisition commands;
\textbf{R3} uses that original pool and \textbf{R4} the corrected

(3/5 each); \texttt{no\_retrieval} reads 1/5 in R3 and 0/5 in R4, and we
quote R4, the corrected pool.  The data-quality correction therefore does
not change the conclusion.

thinking-mode panels named in \secref{sec:protocol}, the combat probe

\section{PlanValidator Pseudocode: Scorer and Runtime Validator}
\label{app:pseudocode}
\label{app:validator}

\secref{sec:planval} of the main text notes that the precondition logic exists twice:
once as the runtime component that gates execution, and once
reimplemented --- single-pass and repair-free --- as the scoring
criterion for the static experiments.  Because the two are not
identical, and because only one of them produced the reported numbers,
we give both rather than a single idealised sketch.

\textbf{Algorithm~1 is the one that matters for every table in this paper.}
It is the \texttt{validatePlanCorrectness} function of the static evaluation
harness, which the re-scoring script imports directly in order to re-validate
every stored seed plan (\appref{app:provenance}).  Algorithm~2 is the deployed
\texttt{PlanValidator} of the agent itself: it runs in the live agent and is
exercised by the live study of \appref{sec:live}, never by the static
evaluations.  Both file paths are given in the code release.

\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
Algorithm 1: static scoring criterion  (validatePlanCorrectness)
Input:  steps[]  -- ordered step array parsed from the model's plan
        inv0     -- starting inventory ([] for the cold-start benchmark)
        goal     -- task goal item and required count
Output: {strict_pass, pvr, issues}

 1. if steps[] is empty: return {strict_pass:false, pvr:false}
 2. A <- multiset(inv0)        // available items; NEVER decremented
 3. issues <- {} ; precsMet <- true ; goalHit <- false
 4. for each step s in steps[]:
 5.   C <- commands(s)          // action_cmd | command | freeformGoal
 6.   (a) declared goal achievement
 7.     if some p in produces(s) has p.item == goal
 8.        and not unrecipedCraftClaim(p, C):  goalHit <- true
 9.   (b) declared preconditions -- items only
10.     for each p in preconditions(s):
11.       if type(p) in {access, station}: continue   // assumed available
12.       if not satisfiedBy(A, p):                   // tool tier inclusive
13.         precsMet <- false ; issues += missing(p)
14.   (c) implicit checks, over a within-step accumulator W <- A
15.     for each cmd in C, in order:
16.       if cmd = collectBlocks(b) and getRequiredTool(b) unsatisfied in W:
17.         issues += tool-gap
18.       if cmd = craftRecipe(x,n) and NO recipe variant of x is
19.          satisfiable from W at ops = ceil(n / outputCount):
20.         issues += ingredient-gap
21.       if cmd = smeltItem(y,n), y correctly named, and W(y) < n:
22.         issues += smelt-input-gap
23.       if cmd = smeltItem(z) with z in {iron,gold,copper}_ore:
24.         issues += naming-gap                       // strict only
25.       W <- W + inferProduces(cmd)
26.   (d) commit step outputs
27.     O <- declared produces(s) UNION inferProduces(cmd) for cmd in C
28.     O <- O minus every unrecipedCraftClaim item
29.     A <- A + O                                     // no consumption
30. if not goalHit and some x in A with x == goal and count >= goalCount:
31.   goalHit <- true
32. if not goalHit: issues += goal-not-produced
33. return {strict_pass: (issues empty) and goalHit,
34.         pvr:         precsMet and goalHit}
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}

Four properties of Algorithm~1 are worth reading off it directly,
because each shapes a reported number.

\emph{The scorer does not model consumption, by design.}  $A$ is only
ever extended (line 29); nothing is subtracted when a step uses a
material.  The metric asks whether the plan gets the prerequisite
\emph{structure} right --- does it acquire each input before it is
needed, name it correctly, and reach the goal --- and deliberately
leaves the material budget to the runtime, where it belongs.  That
division of labour is implemented, not aspirational: when a craft
fails on the bot, \texttt{skills.js} emits
\texttt{Insufficient resources}, \texttt{\_classifyError} maps it to
\texttt{MISSING\_ITEM}, and \texttt{\_routeRecovery} resolves the
shortfall through \texttt{RecipeResolver.resolveDependencyChain},
pushing an acquisition subtask that \texttt{TaskManager} executes
before retrying the step.  Quantity is a closed loop at execution
time, so a static scorer that also charged material budget would be
measuring the planner for something the architecture assigns
elsewhere.  Local quantity errors are still caught where they bite ---
line 19 requires each craft's ingredients to be present at the point of
use, scaled by $\lceil n/\text{outputCount}\rceil$.  What is not
enforced is the aggregate budget across a plan; that is the hand-off to
the runtime replenisher described above, and it is the sense in which a
demonstration or a plan can be ``materially short''
(\appref{app:demoquality}).

\emph{Stations and access preconditions are assumed satisfied}
(line 11).  A plan is never failed for lacking a crafting table or
furnace.  This is deliberate --- the static setting cannot verify
world state --- and it is the single largest respect in which the
scorer is more permissive than the runtime validator, which does check
stations by name.

\emph{Strict and PVR diverge by construction.}  \texttt{precsMet} is
set false only in block~(b), by \emph{declared} preconditions;
\texttt{issues} additionally collects the implicit gaps of block~(c)
and the naming gap of line~24.  PVR therefore measures exactly what
\secref{sec:protocol} says it measures, and the SPR--PVR gap reported throughout the
paper is mechanically the count of tasks whose plans declare a
coherent precondition chain but fail an implicit tool, ingredient,
quantity or naming check.

\emph{The infeasible-task rule is explicit.}
\texttt{unrecipedCraftClaim} (lines 8 and 28) refuses to credit a
declared \texttt{produces} whose only supporting command is a
\texttt{craftRecipe} call for an item with no recipe in
\texttt{minecraft-data} 1.20.4.  This is what makes
\texttt{craft/iron\_horse\_armor} fail in all 102 occurrences
(\appref{app:denom}) rather than passing on a hand-declared
output.  Apart from that rule, declared \texttt{produces} are trusted (lines 7
and 27).  In the 16 primary-study runs they appear in no plan of a
no-retrieval arm and in at most 12 of 180 plans of a retrieval arm, where the
model copies a demonstration's \texttt{Produces:} line, so we re-scored those
runs with every declared field removed: one majority-vote outcome changes in
each GPT-4o retrieval arm (BM25 42$\to$41, \texttt{saer\_cold} 41$\to$40),
none in any other primary-study arm, and no verdict of the pre-specified
family moves.

\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
Algorithm 2: runtime PlanValidator  (deployed agent; live study only)
Input:  steps[], inv0
Output: {valid, gaps}

1. V <- multiset(inv0)                      // virtual inventory
2. for each step s in steps[]:
3.   P <- normalise(preconditions(s))
4.        + inferImplicitPreconditions(cmd) for each cmd in commands(s)
5.   if s contains a smeltItem command:
6.     drop fuel terms from P               // smeltItem selects fuel itself
7.   for each p in P:
8.     if not satisfied(p, V): gaps += p    // access: always satisfied
9.                                          // station: by name
10.                                         // item: name + block-drop alias
11.                                         //       + inclusive tool tier
12.  V <- V - {p in P : p is an item, not a tool, not fuel}   // consumption
13.  D <- declared produces(s) ; I <- inferProduces(cmd) for cmd in commands(s)
14.  if I is non-empty:                     // executability constraint
15.    for each p in D that is an item with p.name not among names(I):
16.      gaps += unsupported-produce(p) ; drop p from D
17.  V <- V + dedup(D + I)
18. return {valid: gaps is empty, gaps}
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}

Algorithm~2 differs from Algorithm~1 in four ways, all of them
consequences of having a real world to act in: it \emph{does} track
consumption (line 12, tools and fuel exempt), it checks station
preconditions rather than assuming them, it exempts fuel on smelting
steps because \texttt{smeltItem} sources its own fuel, and it enforces
an executability constraint (lines 14--16) that rejects a declared
\texttt{produces} no command in the step can generate.  On failure the
enhancer issues one targeted gap-repair regeneration before execution;
that repair path exists only here, and \secref{sec:planval} and Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} state where it applies.  Tool-tier compatibility is inclusive in
both algorithms: a higher tier satisfies any lower requirement.  In
both, implicit requirements resolve against the
\texttt{minecraft-data} 1.20.4 recipe database and the tool-tier
lookup \texttt{RecipeResolver.getRequiredTool}.

We report the divergence rather than present one algorithm as both.
No reported number depends on Algorithm~2: the static tables are
produced by Algorithm~1 alone, and the live study of \appref{sec:live} --- the
only place Algorithm~2 runs --- is reported as a framework-bound
limitation rather than as evidence for the method.

\textbf{What is shared.}  Each model's failing tasks partition into three
groups: those carrying at least one unacquired-prerequisite issue
(precondition, ingredient, tool or smelt-input), those failing only because
the goal item is never produced, and those the majority rule cannot attribute
at all.  Without retrieval the first group holds 41/46 (Qwen3-8B), 31/37
(GPT-4o), 31/35 (Claude Sonnet~4.5), 39/41 (Qwen2.5-Max) and 20/27
(DeepSeek-V3)---74--95\% of failures.  The shared
signature is therefore an \emph{unacquired prerequisite}, not a malformed
or off-goal plan.  Retrieval shrinks both groups by similar proportions: the prerequisite group
falls from 162 to 67 tasks in total (to 30, 16, 8, 8 and 5), and the goal-only
residue from 21 to 10 (5/5/4/2/5 $\to$ 3/2/1/2/2).

The third group is small but real, and we name it rather than fold it into
the second: 0/1/0/0/2 tasks without retrieval and 1/1/0/1/1 with it fail in a
majority of seeds while no \emph{single} class reaches that majority, because
the three seeds fail for different reasons.  A two-way split would absorb
them into ``goal item never produced'', which they need not exhibit at all;
the script asserts the three-way partition sums to the failing-task count and
that the goal-only group never exceeds the goal-not-produced column, so this
cannot silently reappear.

\textbf{What is not shared.}  \emph{Which} prerequisite is missing does
not generalise.  Ingredient gaps run 39 tasks on Qwen3-8B against 3 on
GPT-4o; tool gaps run 33 on Qwen2.5-Max against 2 on Claude Sonnet~4.5.
An earlier draft of this paper asserted the same three failure modes at
every scale; the counts do not support that, and \secref{sec:knowledge_gap}
now states only the shared prerequisite signature.

\textbf{A withdrawn mode.}  The naming-gap column is zero in every model
and both conditions.  The scorer's naming check fires only on
\texttt{smeltItem} applied to an ore name that Minecraft 1.17+ replaced
with a raw-material drop (\appref{app:pseudocode}), and no majority-vote
failure in the primary study trips it.  Version-specific naming was
listed as a failure mode in an earlier draft on the strength of
development-time observations; it is not observable in the reported runs
and we no longer claim it.  Whether it would reappear under a broader
naming check is untested.


\section{Two Analyses of Demonstration Quality}
\label{app:demoquality}

The demonstration bank is the artefact the retrieval conditions differ
over, so its own quality is worth measuring rather than assuming.  Two
analyses do so.  Both are offline, require no model calls, and ship as
runnable scripts; both are post-hoc and sit outside the confirmatory
family of \appref{sec:stats}, with nominal $p$ throughout.  The first
asks whether demonstration quality could explain SAER's edge over BM25
--- it cannot, because SAER is handed the worse demonstrations and
scores higher anyway.  The second isolates a transmission mechanism for
the correctness axis of \secref{sec:knowledge_gap}, on a baseline of
zero in four of the five models: planners copy numeric quantities out of the
demonstrations they are given.

\subsection*{Does Retriever Choice Change Demonstration Quality?}
\label{app:exposure}

If SAER and BM25 differed in how often they retrieve a materially broken
demonstration, that difference would confound \secref{sec:ablation}'s
reading of SAER's edge as a \emph{relevance} effect.  Retrieval is
deterministic given the task and the bank, so the question is decidable
exactly rather than by argument.

\paragraph{What counts as materially broken.}  Re-walk a demonstration
from an empty inventory over an inventory that is \emph{debited} as well
as credited --- each produced unit spendable only once, and a
\texttt{craftRecipe(x,n)} call charged
$\lceil n/\text{outputCount}\rceil$ recipe applications, which is the
conversion the deployed \texttt{skills.js} performs --- and some entries
reach a craft they cannot afford.  On the evaluation-time bank this
holds for 11 of the 25 crafting, 10 of the 21 collecting and 3 of the 9
combat demonstrations.  The static scorer does not charge for the
aggregate material budget across a plan (\appref{app:validator}), so these defects move no credit-only number reported in this paper; they
matter here only as a property of the pool both retrievers draw from.

\paragraph{The comparison.}  We recomputed both selections exactly, with
no model calls
(\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak eval/\allowbreak retriever\_\allowbreak defect\_\allowbreak exposure.mjs}, shipped; it
aborts if the \texttt{setRecipeResolver} defect has regressed, since an
inert resolver would collapse SAER to keyword matching and make the
comparison vacuous).  The two retrievers share only 1.00 of their 3
selections per task on average, so there is ample room for them to
differ.

They do differ, \textbf{and the difference runs against SAER}
(Table~\ref{tab:exposure}).  SAER draws 73 of 180 defective
demonstrations against BM25's 54, and the gap is concentrated at the
top-ranked position (\secref{sec:context}):
SAER's top-ranked demonstration is materially broken on 22 of 60 tasks
against BM25's 8, and the paired split runs entirely one way --- SAER-only
on 14 tasks, BM25-only on \emph{zero}, two-sided $p{=}0.00012$.  The per-task
totals favour BM25 in the same direction but do not reach significance
(26 versus 15 tasks, sign $p{=}0.117$).

\paragraph{Why, and what it implies.}  The mechanism is that SAER is
working as designed.  Its dominant dimension is ingredient overlap at
$\alpha{=}0.50$, which matches material tier by construction, and the
benchmark's L2/L3 tasks are iron- and diamond-tier tools.  In this bank,
tier and brittleness are correlated: 8 of the 10 iron/diamond/gold-tier
crafting demonstrations are materially short, against 3 of the other 15.
SAER therefore retrieves the same-tier demonstration --- the relevant one ---
and in this pool the relevant one is usually the broken one.  The natural
completion is that BM25, matching lexically, is less tightly locked to tier,
but we did not measure its tier-match rate, so that half is a conjecture: the
counts above are equally consistent with BM25 simply selecting more diffusely.
The claim we do make is narrower and the data carries it: the defects are
concentrated in one tier, and all eight of them are the same hardcoded stick
constant, diagnosed below, which the later corrected bank revision repairs.
Separating ``property of the bank'' from ``property of the scorer'' would need
this exposure count recomputed on the corrected revision, which the shipped
scripts support through the \texttt{CRAFTING\_BANK} switch
(\appref{app:overlap}) and we have not run.

The consequence for \secref{sec:ablation} is the opposite of a
confound.  SAER reaches 49/60 against BM25's 42/60 \emph{while} being
fed the materially worse demonstrations, so demonstration quality cannot
be what generates SAER's advantage, and the relevance reading of \secref{sec:ablation} survives this
particular check.  Under the credit-only scorer these shortfalls cost no pass,
so they can work against SAER only under the debited scorer that follows.  The
relevance reading does not survive every scoring choice: the static
scorer credits produced items without debiting them
(\appref{app:validator}), and if material-balance violations are instead
scored as failures, \texttt{saer\_cold} reads 33/60 against BM25's
28/60 and the contrast loses its nominal star
($p{=}0.0156 \rightarrow 0.125$), while BM25 (28) falls below random
(31), inverting the ordering of \secref{sec:ablation}
($p{=}0.375$).  Pooled over the five primary models' arms and the ablation --- the
Gemma4 rows of Table~\ref{tab:main} are not included --- 84 of 413
majority-vote strict passes (20.3\%) carry the violation in a majority of
their passing seeds, and 94 (22.8\%) lose the majority vote once the budget
is charged.  The second set contains the first: the extra ten pass twice
on credit, once cleanly, and fall below $2$ of $3$ when the violating seed is
withdrawn.  The 94 are what Table~\ref{tab:debit_permodel} and the debited
columns of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and of the primary rows of
Table~\ref{tab:main} charge; the 20.3\% is the narrower, more
conservative count
(\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak eval/\allowbreak measure\_\allowbreak consumption\_\allowbreak leniency.mjs},
shipped).  The leniency is not symmetric across conditions, which is why it
matters here rather than as generic evaluator noise: charging the material
budget removes 0 of \texttt{no\_retrieval}'s 19 passes, 7 of
\texttt{random}'s 38, 14 of BM25's 42 and 16 of \texttt{saer\_cold}'s 49.  A
scorer that never debits can only credit plans that produce things, and the
retrieval arms produce more.

\subsection*{Quantity Inheritance from the Demonstration Bank}
\label{app:quantity}

Applied to the bank rather than to the retrievers, the same detector
asks whether the demonstrations are materially executable at all.  They
are not, uniformly, and the shortfalls turn out to be traceable in the
models' own output.

\paragraph{The bank.}  Every failure counted above is the same kind: a
hardcoded quantity in the shared opening --- collect logs, craft planks,
craft sticks, craft a wooden pickaxe --- that does not scale with the
chain the demonstration then walks.  \texttt{skills.js} reads these
arguments as output-item counts and converts them to
$\lceil n/\text{yield}\rceil$ recipe applications, so the constant fixes
the budget exactly.  The two banks fail on different constants.  In the
crafting bank, 9 of the 11 failures are \emph{sticks}: the
\texttt{stick} argument takes only the values 2 and 4 in this revision,
and \texttt{craftRecipe('stick', 4)} is a single application yielding
four, whereas a demonstration that crafts a wooden, then a stone, then
an iron tool needs six.  \texttt{crafting/iron\_axe} is representative:
it reaches its final craft with zero sticks against the two an iron axe
requires.  In the collecting bank 8 of the 10 failures are
\emph{planks}: 9 of its 17 \texttt{oak\_planks} calls ask for 4, against
3 of 25 in the crafting bank, and four planks cannot cover a crafting
table (4), sticks (2) and a pickaxe (3).  The later corrected revision,
supplied alongside the evaluated one, raises the stick constant to 6 and
8 --- among other edits, which \appref{app:overlap} describes from the
ingredient-set side --- and that is what takes its material-shortfall
count to 1 of 25 rather than 11.  The recipe graph is available to neither
the demonstration author nor, as the next paragraph shows, the model
that reads it.

\paragraph{The transfer.}  Because the two banks carry different
constants, the constant a model emits is a fingerprint of what it read.
Table~\ref{tab:quantity} counts the \texttt{oak\_planks} argument in
every generated plan for the 11 COLLECT tasks, which retrieve from the
collecting bank.  Under \texttt{no\_retrieval} four of the five primary
models emit the sufficient 12 in \emph{every} trial --- 0 of 17, 0 of
18, 0 of 8 and 0 of 15 uses of the insufficient 4.  The fifth is the
exception that bounds the argument: Qwen3-8B parsed only 2 COLLECT plans
without retrieval and both used 4, so its baseline is 2/2 rather than
zero, and the contrast below is a four-model one.  Under retrieval the
insufficient value appears: 10 of 12 trials on Qwen2.5-Max, 13 of 13 on
Qwen3-8B, 4 of 15 on DeepSeek-V3 and 2 of 17 on GPT-4o.  The same models keep emitting 12 on
CRAFT tasks.  The crafting bank is not uniform either --- its 25 planks calls
read 12 eleven times, 8 ten times, 4 three times and 20 once --- so what
separates the two categories is how often the \emph{insufficient} 4 appears:
3 of 25 calls against the collecting bank's 9 of 17.  On CRAFT the models
therefore emit a sufficient value that a majority of their retrieved
demonstrations also carry; on COLLECT they emit the short one that a majority
carries.  Random retrieval transmits it too --- 6 of its 12 COLLECT trials on
Qwen2.5-Max, a condition Table~\ref{tab:quantity} does not tabulate --- as it
must, since it
draws from the same bank --- so this is a property of the demonstration pool,
not of SAER's ranking.

\paragraph{What this establishes.}  It supplies the transmission
mechanism behind the correctness axis of \secref{sec:knowledge_gap},
which the 35-task contrast of \secref{sec:knowledge_gap} shows only through
outcomes --- SAER trails random until broken L2 demonstrations are repaired.
Here it is operating, on a baseline of zero in the four models that have one:
the planner reproduces a numeric argument it read, and the argument is
wrong.  It
also explains why the architecture assigns quantity to the runtime
rather than to the planner --- these models demonstrably do not check a
copied constant against the recipe graph, so something downstream has
to.  The models differ: Claude Sonnet~4.5 overrides the
demonstration in all 16 trials, Qwen3-8B in none of 13.  None of the
copied quantities changes a credit-only number, since that scorer does not
charge for them (\appref{app:validator}), which is why they survived to
be found.

We claim no dose--response from this: it does not follow that a bank's
defect rate predicts how often plans in the corresponding category are
themselves short, and plan length is an uncontrolled confound
throughout, since longer chains offer more chances to under-budget
regardless of what was retrieved.  What the fingerprint does establish,
on a baseline of zero in four of the five models, is that these planners copy numeric
arguments out of retrieved demonstrations without checking them against
the recipe graph, and that a single wrong constant in one demonstration
file propagates to most trials of every task that file serves.  It also
bounds the bank audit of \appref{app:traindata}, which verified that
each declared precondition is produced by an earlier step: that is a
statement about the \emph{presence} of an item, not about the
\emph{amount}.  The bank is short on amounts, and the audit was not
designed to see it.

\section{Oracle Categories and the Deployment Routing Path}
\label{app:intent_oracle}

The deployed pipeline classifies a request with an LLM call
(\texttt{getIntent()}, \secref{sec:catfilter}) and admits only
demonstrations from the matching file.  \secref{sec:catfilter}
enumerates the labels the classifier in the code release emits.  The
static harness does \emph{not} run that classifier.  Instead it reads
the ground-truth \texttt{category} field of the frozen task list, at two
places in \texttt{scripts/eval/static\_plan\_eval.js}: the per-task loop
calls \texttt{loadTrainingData(task.\-category)} to pick the
demonstration file, and \texttt{generatePlan} builds the request object
with fields \texttt{input: task.prompt} and
\texttt{intent: task.category}, whose
\texttt{intent} field is emitted verbatim into the prompt by
\texttt{FewShotLearning.\-buildRequestAnalysisSection} as an
\texttt{Intent Type:} line.  All reported numbers are therefore an
oracle-category upper bound, and we state this rather than leave it to
be inferred from the release.

\paragraph{What it does not bias.}  Both injection points sit
\emph{above} the \texttt{switch} over conditions.  The request object is
constructed once and passed unchanged to every branch, and the
demonstration pool is loaded once per task before the branch is
selected.  Consequently \texttt{no\_retrieval} carries the same
\texttt{Intent Type:} line as \texttt{saer\_cold} --- it simply carries
no examples --- and \texttt{random\_retrieval},
\texttt{bm25\_retrieval} and \texttt{saer\_cold} all sample from the
identical, oracle-selected candidate pool.  No condition receives a
category signal the others lack.  The oracle can therefore raise or
lower all conditions together, but it cannot move the paired differences
between them, and every claim in this paper is stated as such a
difference (McNemar on paired per-task outcomes, never on a single
condition's absolute rate).  The retriever itself never reads the label:
\texttt{SAERScorer} contains no reference to \texttt{intent}, and its
four component scores are computed from ingredients, tokens, tool tiers
and difficulty alone.

\paragraph{A second candidate, checked and ruled out.}  The category is one
place the frozen task list could leak into retrieval; the difficulty tier is
the other, because $\delta{\cdot}L$ of \secref{sec:scoring} needs a level for
the \emph{query} and the task list carries one.  It does not come from there.
\texttt{SAERScorer.estimateLevel} takes only the request string and matches it
against four ordered keyword lists, defaulting to 2, and the harness passes it
nothing else (\texttt{input: task.prompt}).  Evaluated against the frozen
list's own tier labels it agrees on \textbf{20 of 60} tasks --- the same score
a constant ``L2'' answer gets, since 20 of the 60 are L2 --- with 41 of the 60
estimated as L2 and the confusions running both ways (12 L3 tasks read as L2,
6 L2 tasks as L1).  So the fourth dimension is a weak surface heuristic, not a
second oracle, and the tier it scores against is not the tier the benchmark
stratifies on.

\paragraph{What it does bias.}  Deployment fidelity.  If
\texttt{getIntent()} misclassifies a request, the wrong demonstration
file is loaded and \emph{all} retrieval conditions degrade towards the
no-retrieval floor, because none of them can rank an example that was
never a candidate.  The size of that degradation is a product of two
quantities: the classifier's error rate over the 60 tasks, and the
conditional loss given an error.  We measured the first, which makes the
second moot here.  Running the deployed classifier over the frozen task
list --- \texttt{determineIntentType} invoked on its shipped prompt and
parser, DeepSeek-V3, three seeds, 180 calls, zero API failures --- gives
\textbf{100\% routing accuracy} at the task level (60/60 under the same
majority vote used throughout), with no task misrouted; trial-level
label accuracy in that run was 99.4\%, and every label-discordant trial
still routed to the same demonstration file as its ground truth, so its
conditional loss was zero.  These are figures from one dated run against
a live commercial endpoint rather than a deterministic quantity:
trial-level counts, and which particular tasks come out seed-unstable,
move between runs.  We therefore rest the argument on the majority-vote
routing result, which is the quantity the oracle bound actually
concerns, and on that quantity the bound is tight on this benchmark: no
reported number would change under the deployed router.

We claim this for the 60 held-out prompts only.  They are short and
templated (``craft a wooden sword'', ``kill a creeper''), so the result
bounds how optimistic the oracle is \emph{here}; it is not evidence that
the classifier is robust on the free-form chat the deployed agent also
accepts, which remains unmeasured.  Two further limits are worth
stating.  The model was served from the first-party DeepSeek endpoint
rather than the third-party path of the deployed profile, which serves
the same \texttt{deepseek-v3} model.  And routing accuracy is measured
against the oracle \texttt{category} field, so it inherits whatever
labelling convention that field encodes.  The measurement script is
\texttt{scripts/eval/intent\_accuracy.mjs}; it reaches the deployed
prompt and parser through the class prototype rather than restating
them, so it cannot drift from the shipped classifier.

\paragraph{A structural routing defect, found and fixed.}  Auditing the
classifier against the oracle exposed a defect that the oracle had been
masking, and we report it rather than leave it in the release.  The
classifier's category list folded combat into the movement category
(\emph{``EXPLORE --- combat, movement, or exploration''}), and a
separate hardcoded whitelist inside the response parser enumerated
labels that likewise excluded \texttt{combat}, coercing any
\texttt{combat} verdict to the \texttt{general} fallback.  The
intent-to-file map, meanwhile, already contained a
\texttt{combat}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{combat\_examples.json} entry.  The
two ends therefore disagreed, and \texttt{combat\_examples.json} was
unreachable from the deployed path: a request such as ``attack a
zombie'' was routed either to the collecting demonstrations, through the
movement label, or to the crafting demonstrations, through the
\texttt{general} default.  The frozen benchmark contains 5 combat tasks
among 44 \texttt{craft} and 11 \texttt{collect}, so under deployment
those five could not have been routed correctly by construction --- had
the deployed router been in the loop at all, which for the static
results it was not.  The node-level pipeline figure in the code release
records this defect: the label set drawn there is the pre-fix deployment one,
and it carries no combat entry.

The code release fixes this: \texttt{combat} is now a first-class
category with its own description and keywords, movement and exploration
are described separately, and the parser's whitelist is derived from the
intent-to-file map rather than restated, so the two can no longer drift
apart.  A regression test (\texttt{tests/intent\_routing.test.js}, 34
assertions) asserts the invariant directly: every label the map can
route is enumerated in the classification prompt, survives the parser
without coercion, and does not resolve to the crafting default.
\secref{sec:catfilter} describes the fixed classifier.

\paragraph{No reported number changes.}  The fix cannot move any result
in this paper, because the static harness never calls
\texttt{getIntent()} --- it reads the \texttt{category} field of the
frozen task list, and that field was already \texttt{combat} for the
five combat tasks, which already loaded
\texttt{combat\_examples.json}.  The defect lived exclusively on the
deployment path.  What it does change is the interpretation: before the
fix, the combat results were an existence probe under a router that
could not have reproduced them in deployment; after it, the deployed
router can reach the same demonstration file the evaluation used.  The
routing measurement above closes the half of this gap that concerns
label accuracy: the deployed router reaches the correct demonstration
file for all five combat tasks, as it does for all 60.  What remains
unmeasured is the other half --- an end-to-end re-run of the main table
under predicted rather than oracle categories, which would also carry
the classifier's own latency and failure modes.  With routing accuracy
at 60/60 that re-run is expected to reproduce the reported numbers, but
we report the expectation as such rather than as a result.  Neither half
bears on the knowledge-bottleneck claims, which are contrastive.

\paragraph{Three branches are nominal rather than studied.}  The frozen
benchmark is 44 \texttt{craft}, 11 \texttt{collect} and 5
\texttt{combat} tasks; it contains no \texttt{cook}, \texttt{build} or
\texttt{explore} task, and no result anywhere in this paper exercises
those branches.  They exist in the router because the agent accepts
free-form chat, not because they are part of the evaluated system, and
the defect described above is correspondingly a deployment-path defect
only.  We report it because the code release is an artefact readers may
run, not because it bears on a measurement.

\paragraph{Demonstration command validity, disclosed but not repaired.}
The command validator shipped with the release checked only the crafting
and collecting files.  Extending it to every routed file --- combat and
build had never been checked, combat because it was unreachable ---
reports 25 invalid commands: 13 in the combat file, 9 in the build file
and 3 in the crafting file.  One defect dominates: 13 uses of
\texttt{!equipItem}, which is not in the bot command registry (the
registered name is \texttt{!equip}), spread across the combat and build
files.  The remainder are 4 uses of \texttt{planks} where a specific
wood type is required, 2 \texttt{!startBuild} calls, 3
\texttt{!placeHere} calls naming an item rather than a placeable block
(\texttt{flint\_and\_steel}, \texttt{water\_bucket}), and 3 counts of a
single \texttt{!attack} call missing its second argument.  We report these rather than repair them.  The
demonstration bank is the frozen artefact behind every reported number,
and editing it after the fact would fork the artefact from the results;
the validator extension is a script change and touches no data.

Reachability is not uniform, so we measured it.  The build file is never
retrieved, since no benchmark task carries that category.  The three
crafting errors are confined to a single demonstration,
\texttt{crafting/bow}, which the retriever selects for none of the 44
craft tasks.  The combat file is the exception: all 9 of its
demonstrations contain \texttt{!equipItem}, and all 5 combat tasks
retrieve at least one of them, so every combat prompt under every
retrieval condition carries the invalid command.

\paragraph{Measuring demonstration-defect inheritance.}  That
contamination propagates, and the propagation is measurable --- which
makes this the one part of this appendix that is a finding rather than a
disclosure.  Because the defect sits in \emph{every} retrieval
condition's prompt and in \emph{no} no-retrieval prompt, the gap between
the two arms isolates how often a planner copies a broken command
straight out of its demonstrations.  Scanning every stored per-seed
result file under \texttt{scripts/eval/results} (308 files, superseded
runs included) for the string \texttt{equipItem} in the generated plan
of a \texttt{combat/} task: it appears in \emph{0} of 505 no-retrieval
trials, and in 726 of 1{,}016 retrieval trials (71.5\%) --- 72.9\% under
\texttt{saer\_cold}, 74.2\% under BM25, 61.2\% under random.  A model
given no demonstrations never invents this command; a model given these
demonstrations copies it verbatim about three times in four.  The rate
is the stable quantity here: an earlier, narrower sweep, whose file set is
not recoverable, also gave 71\%, and the per-condition rates move by at most
two points across sweep widths while the no-retrieval count is exactly
zero at every width.  We therefore ship the scan rather than the counts:
\texttt{scripts/eval/scan\_demo\_defect\_inheritance.mjs} states its
file set explicitly and asserts the zero.

The inheritance is invisible to the metric because the static scorer
infers production only from \texttt{craftRecipe}, \texttt{smeltItem},
collection and \texttt{attack} commands (an attack credits five units of the mob's
drop, which is how a combat task reaches its goal item, e.g.\ \texttt{gunpowder}
for \texttt{combat/kill\_creeper}); an equip step
produces nothing under either spelling, so a wrong one is neither
penalised nor rewarded.  Consistently, no validation issue in any
scanned combat trial mentions \texttt{equip}.  The reported combat
numbers are therefore unaffected, and the probe of
\appref{sec:combat_probe} remains a statement about plan structure.  But
the plans it scores as correct contain a command that would fail on
contact with the bot, which is a concrete instance of the gap between
this static metric and execution that \appref{sec:live} discusses in the
aggregate.

\paragraph{Scope of these fixes.}  None of them can move a reported
number, and the reason is structural rather than argued: the static
harness \texttt{scripts/eval/static\_plan\_eval.js} does not import the
enhancer module at all.  It constructs \texttt{FewShotLearning}, \texttt{BM25Retriever} and
\texttt{RecipeResolver} directly, scores plans with its own validator, and carries its own intent-to-file
table.  The classifier and the routing table described above are
reachable only from the deployed agent path.

\section{A Separate Live Probe: Structured Fact Sharing Between Two Agents}
\label{sec:multiagent}
\label{app:crossagent}
\label{app:multiagent}
\label{app:twostage}
\label{app:two_stages}
\label{app:implicit}
\label{app:sharedmemory}

\noindent\textbf{No claim of this paper rests on this appendix.}  It reports a
two-bot live study of the \emph{second} retrieval stage of the same enhancer,
kept so that the released system can be read end to end and because the
numbers were collected under this project.  It exercises none of the
components varied in the main study: no SAER retrieval, no plan validator, no
executor from Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.  The node-level pipeline figure, the
per-trial rows, the contact sheets of the 18 screen recordings and the scripts
that re-derive every number below ship with the code release (under
\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak knowledge\_reuse/}; the figure under
\texttt{docs/\allowbreak paper/\allowbreak neurips\_ws/\allowbreak figures/}).

\paragraph{The second stage.}  The procedural stage (SAER) filters
demonstrations by intent category, ranks them by ingredient, keyword and level
overlap, and places the top three before the planning call.  The team stage
has the same shape over a different record: when more than one bot is active,
a shared store (\texttt{SharedMemory}) holds \emph{world facts} (source agent,
resource, position, confidence, timestamps, optional expiry); a query filters
them by namespace, record type, resource, expiry, age and radius, ranks them by
source confidence discounted by distance, and places the result in the
consumer's prompt immediately before its single selection call.  Neither stage
updates any weights.  The correspondence is one of shape, not shared code.
Only the world-fact write/read path (\texttt{recordWorldFact()} /
\texttt{queryWorldFacts()}) is on the measured route; the store's reservations,
supply requests and work ledger are implemented but not measured here.

\paragraph{Design.}  Three model endpoints (Doubao-Seed-2.0-Mini
\texttt{doubao-seed-2-0-mini-260428}, a hosted \texttt{qwen-max} endpoint, and
Qwen3-8B served locally through Ollama as \texttt{qwen3:8b}) $\times$ three
sharing conditions --- none (\emph{normal}), free-form chat
(\emph{explicit}), structured fact sharing (\emph{implicit}) --- $\times$ two
tasks (\emph{geothermal}: collect soul sand from the one valid site;
\emph{workstation}: reach a discovered crafting table and craft a wooden
pickaxe) $\times$ five trials per cell, 90 trials in all.  A scout follows a
fixed route and finds the target at its second inspection, so the discovery
moment is constant; target identity rotates across attempts and is identical
within each normal/explicit/implicit triplet.  Under \emph{normal} the consumer
has no information from the scout and searches three candidate sites; under
\emph{explicit} the scout reports over chat, which the consumer must parse;
under \emph{implicit} the consumer reads the structured fact directly.  The
store is rebuilt from empty for every attempt, and \emph{normal} and
\emph{explicit} never touch it.  Each trial issues one scripted model call (two
under \emph{explicit}: report and interpretation).  Before every attempt both
agents' inventories and memories are cleared and the terrain reset;
teleportation is allowed only before the trial announcement.

\paragraph{Results.}  Success is saturated for \emph{normal} and
\emph{implicit} (30/30; Table~\ref{tab:multiagent}), so the signal is overhead.  \emph{Implicit} removes
all 22 redundant consumer inspections and uses 30 model calls against
\emph{explicit}'s 60; both are exact counts over the recorded trials, and the
call reduction follows from the protocol rather than from model behaviour, so
neither carries a test.  The tests belong to post-discovery duration: paired
\emph{implicit}$-$\emph{normal} is $-4.85$\,s (bootstrap 95\% CI
$[-6.68,-3.05]$; 26/30 faster; two-sided sign test
$p{=}5.95{\times}10^{-5}$), and \emph{implicit}$-$\emph{explicit} is
$-1.33$\,s ($[-2.16,-0.52]$; 24/30 faster; $p{=}0.00143$).  \emph{Explicit}'s
two failures both occur when the consumer selects a different site from the
one the report names.  The pooled speed-up is not uniform across endpoints
(Table~\ref{tab:kr_permodel}): against \emph{normal} it is significant on both
hosted APIs (10/10 faster each, $p{=}0.002$) but not on the locally served 8B
model (6/10, $p{=}0.75$), and against \emph{explicit} its interval excludes
zero only for Doubao.  Intervals are percentile intervals from 20{,}000 paired
bootstrap resamples; because the five attempts per cell reuse two scenario
templates, the inference describes the recorded protocol rather than a task
population.  All contrasts lie outside the confirmatory family of
\appref{sec:stats}.

\paragraph{Integrity.}  An automated audit over the retained records passes
all 18 programmed checks: exactly 90 trials, 5 per cell; all 30 triplets
target-matched; 90 valid reset audits and 90 unique per-trial state paths;
60 of 60 \emph{explicit} messages observed and model-generated, against none
under \emph{implicit}; a structured-fact create-and-consume trace in all 30
\emph{implicit} trials; and no work-progress teleportation.  No prompt in any
condition contains the words \emph{duplicate}, \emph{overlap},
\emph{division of labor}, \emph{role assignment} or \emph{assigned role}, so
the coordination observed is not instructed.  The shipped logs have absolute
paths replaced, so integrity checks should use
\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak CHECKSUMS\_AS\_SHIPPED.txt}, not the run-time
manifest's hashes.

\paragraph{What this does not test.}  Whether a procedural remedy and a team
remedy compose.  That needs a factorial crossing procedural retrieval (on/off)
with team context (on/off) on tasks that require both a deep dependency chain
and a discovery handoff, which we have not run.  A separate 120-trial
allocation benchmark that touches the work-ledger path is not reported here
and carries no claim.
